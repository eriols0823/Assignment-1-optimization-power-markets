Battery sizing & profits: what the sweeps showed
The profit of a price-taking battery against its energy capacity and power limit — piecewise linear, with the Lagrange multipliers as the slopes — and what that says about how to size a battery for a given price profile.

LEARNING OBJECTIVES
Objective 1 Read the profit sensitivity to Ē and P̄ from a sweep, and locate the kinks where a new constraint starts or stops binding Objective 2 Explain why the slope of the profit curve equals the sum of the multipliers of the swept bound, and why it is valid only until the next kink Objective 3 Use the duration D = Ē/P̄ against the price-block length H, and the multiplier sums Σt δ̄t, Σt μ̄t, as the sizing and investment signals

1The activity, in one paragraph

In pairs, on tab C1 of the dashboard, you swept the energy capacity Ē and the power limit P̄ of the self-scheduling battery of the lecture (no losses, starts empty, free end) and read the sum of the Lagrange multipliers next to the profit curve — on the four-hour toy (15 kWh / 10 kW, prices 20, 30, 60, 80 DKK/kWh), on the 24-hour class day (25 kWh / 10 kW), and on two contrasting price profiles. The four tasks were:

  1. Toy: sweep Ē from 0 to 30 kWh at P̄ = 10 kW. Where does the profit stop growing, and why? Read Σt δ̄t next to the curve.
  2. Toy: sweep P̄ from 0 to 25 kW at Ē = 15 kWh. Same questions; read Σt (μ̄cht + μ̄dist).
  3. 24-h class day (Ē = 25 kWh, P̄ = 10 kW): repeat sweep 1. At which Ē does 'profit per kWh' start to fall? Compare with the length of the cheap and expensive blocks.
  4. Profiles 'one evening peak' then 'square wave H = 2 h': which of Ē or P̄ matters more on each — and why?
Why sweeps and multipliers belong together

Changing Ē or P̄ and re-solving gives the profit as a function of the parameter — a sensitivity. The LP duality of Week 4 says the same thing without re-solving: the derivative of the optimal profit with respect to a bound is the multiplier of that bound, here summed over the hours in which it binds. The sweep is the numerical check of the theory; the multipliers are the theory reading the sweep. Where they disagree — at a kink — both one-sided derivatives are correct, one to the left and one to the right.

2What you should have seen, task by task

Task 1 — sweep Ē on the toy: the slope is Σt δ̄t

Navy: profit; blue dashed: Σt δ̄t, the sum of the multipliers of the energy bound (𝒫8). Pink dotted lines: the kinks at 10 and 20 kWh; red: the preset (15 kWh). Between kinks the blue step equals the slope of the profit; exactly at a kink the solver reports one of the two one-sided values.

The profit is piecewise linear. From 0 to 10 kWh every kWh of capacity is bought at 20 and sold at 80: the slope is 60, and Σt δ̄t = 60 because the battery is full in hours 1, 2 and 3 and the bound (𝒫8) binds in all of them. From 10 to 20 kWh the 20 → 80 trade is capped by the power limit (10 kW for one hour), so every further kWh is bought at 30 and sold at 60: the slope, and Σt δ̄t, step down to 30. At 20 kWh the profit stops growing: with two cheap hours at 10 kW, no more than 20 kWh can be bought in a day, so the power limit takes over and Σt δ̄t drops to 0. The profit caps at 900 DKK.

Task 2 — sweep P̄ on the toy: the slope is Σt (μ̄cht + μ̄dist)

Navy: profit; blue dashed: the sum of the power-bound multipliers (𝒫4), (𝒫6). Kinks at 7.5 and 15 kW; the preset is 10 kW.

Up to 7.5 kW every kW is used four times — buy at 20 and at 30, sell at 60 and at 80 — so one more kW earns (80 − 20) + (60 − 30) = 90 DKK per day, and Σt μ̄t = 90. Beyond 7.5 kW the 15 kWh of energy capacity are already filled by two charging hours, so an extra kW only shifts energy from the 30 → 60 trade to the 20 → 80 one: slope 30. At 15 kW the whole capacity is bought in one hour and sold in one hour; more power is worthless and the sum is 0. Same picture as task 1 with the roles of the two limits exchanged.

Task 3 — the class day: profit per kWh falls once D exceeds the price blocks

Navy: profit per kWh of capacity; blue dashed: Σt δ̄t; preset 25 kWh (D = 2.5 h). The class day has a 6-hour cheap night, a 4-hour morning peak, a 6-hour cheap afternoon and a 6-hour evening peak.

Profit per kWh is flat at 121 DKK/kWh·day up to 10 kWh (D = 1 h: only the best hour of each block is used), then falls gently — 113 at 20 kWh, 107 at 25 kWh, 103 at 30 kWh — while the duration stays inside the 4- to 6-hour price blocks, and steeply once it does not: 92 at 40 kWh, 79 at 50 kWh, 52 at 80 kWh. The multiplier sum tells the same story in steps of 10 kWh — one more hour of charging and discharging each: 121, 106, 82 (at the 25-kWh preset), 57, 31, 20, and 0 from 60 kWh on, where the battery can no longer be filled and emptied within the blocks. The kink to look for sits where D ≈ H: a battery should be about as long as the price event it lives on, and no longer.

Task 4 — two profiles, two answers

One evening peak — energy matters

A long cheap night and a six-hour expensive evening. At 25 kWh / 10 kW the multiplier sums are Σt δ̄t = 60 DKK per kWh·day against Σt μ̄t = 20 DKK per kW·day; sweeping Ē keeps adding profit all the way to 80 kWh (still 15 DKK per kWh at the end), and profit per kWh drifts slowly from 70 to 45 with no cliff. Every kWh finds an hour of the long peak to be sold in: the maximum state of charge matters most.

Square wave H = 2 h — power matters

Two cheap hours, two expensive hours, six times a day. At 25 kWh / 10 kW the battery can only move 20 kWh per block: Σt δ̄t = 0 and Σt μ̄t = 720 DKK per kW·day. Sweeping Ē does nothing beyond 20 kWh; sweeping P̄ adds 720 DKK per kW up to 12.5 kW, where the 25 kWh become the limit. Short peaks reward power: the maximum power matters most.

3Duration decides, size only rescales

The four toy batteries of the solution slide, normalised per kWh and per kW:

Battery (toy) Ē (kWh) P̄ (kW) D = Ē/P̄ (h) Profit (DKK) per kWh per kW cycles captured spread
base 15 10 1.5 750 50 75 1.0 50
double Ē 30 10 3 900 30 90 0.67 45
double both 30 20 1.5 1500 50 75 1.0 50
double P̄ 15 20 0.75 900 60 45 1.0 60
  • Same D → same profit per kWh and per kW (rows 1 and 3): doubling both limits doubles the profit and changes nothing else. The prices see a battery through its duration.
  • Longer D → lower profit per kWh (row 2): energy waits for spreads that are not there; the extra 15 kWh only reach the 30 → 60 trade, and the two cheap hours at 10 kW cap what can be bought at 20 kWh. Cycles and captured spread both fall.
  • Shorter D → higher per kWh, lower per kW (row 4): power waits for energy; the second 10 kW only reshuffle the same 15 kWh into the best hours.
  • Profit = cycles × Ē × captured spread, so any change in Ē or P̄ shows up in one of the two factors — which is what the sweeps let you read.
The sizing rule

Match the duration D to the length H of the price event the battery lives on — as long as the peak, and no longer. Losses do not change the rule; they set a minimum spread λhigh ρch ρdis > λlow below which the battery does nothing whatever its size.

4The multipliers are the signal

What you measured by sweeping is what the dual solution states without a single re-solve:

One more kWh of capacity

∂profit / ∂Ē = Σt δ̄t

Worth δ̄t in every hour the battery is full — the jump of ψ at that hour. Toy: 30 DKK per kWh·day at the preset, until Ē = 20 kWh where the power limit takes over (then 0). Class day: 82 DKK per kWh·day at 25 kWh.

One more kW of power

∂profit / ∂P̄ = Σt (μ̄cht + μ̄dist)

Worth μ̄t in every saturated hour — the gap between the price and the value of stored energy, λt − ψt when discharging, ψt − λt when charging. Toy: 10 + 20 = 30 DKK per kW·day; class day: 63.

Both are slopes, valid until the next kink: the multiplier tells you what the next unit is worth, not the average value of the units you already have, and it is zero as soon as the other limit binds. At a kink the two one-sided re-solves disagree and the solver reports one of them; both are correct sensitivities, for the two directions.

The investment reading — Week 4's zero-profit logic, one multiplier per resource

Invest in kWh while Σt δ̄t per day beats the daily annualised cost of a kWh of capacity; invest in kW while Σt μ̄t per day beats that of a kW of power. Keep adding the resource whose rent exceeds its cost, and stop at the kink where it no longer does — the same argument that told you last week what a power plant is worth.

5Common misreadings

Heard in the room What the numbers say
"The multiplier is the average value of a kWh." It is the marginal one: the toy earns 50 DKK per kWh on average at 15 kWh but the 16th kWh earns 30, and the 21st earns nothing.
"Doubling the battery doubles the profit." Only if D is kept: doubling Ē alone gives 900, not 1500, because cycles and captured spread both fall.
"A bigger battery is always better on a real day." Profit per kWh falls as soon as D exceeds the price blocks; above 60 kWh at 10 kW the class-day battery cannot even be filled and emptied within them.
"Σt δ̄t = 0 means capacity is worthless." It means the other limit binds: on the square wave the 25-kWh battery is power-limited, and one more kW is worth 720 DKK per day.
"The dashboard multiplier disagrees with my re-solve." You are standing on a kink (Ē = 20 kWh on the toy): the left and right derivatives differ, and the solver returns one of them.

6Takeaways

  1. The optimal profit is piecewise linear in every bound of the LP; each kink is a constraint starting or stopping to bind.

  2. The slope of the profit against Ē is Σt δ̄t; against P̄ it is Σt (μ̄cht + μ̄dist). A sweep measures what the duals state.

  3. Duration decides, size rescales: match D = Ē/P̄ to the length H of the price event. Short peaks reward power, long peaks reward energy.

  4. A multiplier is a marginal value, valid until the next kink — never an average, and zero once the other limit binds.

  5. The investment signal is one multiplier per resource: add capacity while its daily rent beats its daily annualised cost.
