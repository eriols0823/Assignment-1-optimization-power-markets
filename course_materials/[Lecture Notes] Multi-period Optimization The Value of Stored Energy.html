1From one period to many: the same duality, a new coupling

In week 4, a self-scheduling unit answered one question per hour: given the price , produce or not? Its opportunity cost came from a scarce capacity in that hour. A battery cannot decide hour by hour. Every kWh it sells has to be bought earlier, and every kWh it buys can only be sold later: the state of charge links the hours. The duality machinery is exactly the same as in weeks 3 and 4 — what is new is the coupling constraint, and its multiplier turns out to be a price on stored energy.

Answer. D. The four candidates earn A (+10, 0, 0, −10): 600 DKK · B (+10, −10, +10, −10): 300 DKK · C (+10, +5, −10, −5): 650 DKK · D (+10, +5, −5, −10): 750 DKK. The common wrong answer is A: it forgets that a 15-kWh battery holds one and a half 10-kW trades.

Solving it by hand: a merit order of price spreads

Without losses, a kWh bought in hour and sold in hour earns the spread . The optimal schedule fills these pairs in decreasing order of spread — a merit order, as in week 4, but over pairs of hours rather than units — until a power or energy limit stops it:

  • Best spread: hour 1 → hour 4, 80 − 20 = 60 DKK/kWh. Charge and discharge as much as possible: the power limit stops it at 10 kWh. Profit 600 DKK.
  • Next-best available spread: hour 2 → hour 3, 60 − 30 = 30 DKK/kWh. The pairs 1 → 3 and 2 → 4 would reuse a saturated hour. Only 15 − 10 = 5 kWh of room is left, so the energy limit stops it at 5 kWh. Profit 150 DKK.
  • Every remaining pair is either blocked by a limit or has a non-positive spread. Total: 750 DKK.
Hour (DKK/kWh) Action end of hour (kWh) Limit reached
1 20 charge 10 kW 10 charging power
2 30 charge 5 kW 15 energy (full)
3 60 discharge 5 kW 10 —
4 80 discharge 10 kW 0 discharging power, energy (empty)

Why not discharge more in hour 3?

Hour 3 pays 60 DKK/kWh and neither a power nor an energy limit binds there, so the battery could sell more. It does not, because the 10 kWh it keeps are sold in hour 4 at 80 DKK/kWh. Energy sold at 60 is energy that cannot be sold later at 80: this is the intertemporal opportunity cost, and it is what the battery compares the current price with — not the price itself, and not an average price.

Remember this one

A storage decision is never about one hour. The price at which it is worth selling a kWh now is set by the best other hour in which the same kWh could be sold (or bought). Block B turns this sentence into a multiplier.

The intermediate formulation (no end-of-horizon condition yet)

Profit. Cash flow from energy sold minus energy purchased, at the given prices.
State-of-charge dynamics. The only constraint that links hours: what is in the battery at the end of hour is what was there before, plus what was stored, minus what was taken out. Charging stores less than was bought; discharging takes out — more than was sold.
Power bounds. Exactly the week 4 bounds, one pair per direction and per hour.
Energy bounds. The battery can be neither emptier than empty nor fuller than full.
Structure

The problem is a linear program: linear objective, linear constraints, continuous variables. Whenever it is feasible, (weak) Slater's condition holds, so strong duality holds and the KKT conditions are necessary and sufficient. The hand solution of the toy is an LP solution, and section 5 can read it through its multipliers.

The modelling choices behind the formulation

  • Price-taker. The prices are given: the battery is too small to move them. A large storage fleet would flatten the spreads it lives on.
  • Perfect foresight within the horizon. All prices are known when the schedule is decided; what happens after is the subject of section 4.
  • Linear losses, no degradation cost. Efficiencies are constant; cycling the battery costs nothing beyond the energy lost.
  • No binary to prevent simultaneous charging and discharging. Harmless while prices are positive — with losses, doing both at once only wastes energy — but worth revisiting with negative prices.

4What the model assumes at the end of the horizon

The intermediate formulation says nothing about the hours after . That silence is itself an assumption, and it drives the schedule in the last hours.

Poll — predict first

With the intermediate formulation, if the battery starts full, at the end of the day it will be…

Answer. Empty. Nothing in the model rewards energy left at , so every kWh that can be sold at a positive price before is sold — here 5 kWh in hour 3 and 10 kWh in hour 4, for 1 100 DKK. The model behaves as if the world ended at : it dumps at the end.

Appropriate

An empty battery at is right when next-day prices start low: sell today, buy again tomorrow.

Costly

It is costly when next-day prices start high: the energy would have been worth more tomorrow than it earned today.

Key insight

Every end-of-horizon condition is an assumption about the prices after . The ‘right’ is a forecast in disguise: day 1 always loses from keeping energy, and whether day 2 pays it back depends on information the one-day model does not have. Section 8 compares ways of making that assumption explicit.
