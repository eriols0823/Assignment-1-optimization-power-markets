Problem statement

The objective of this assignment is to model and analyze an active distribution network with flexible residential consumers. You will build consumer-level flexibility models, analyze the structure of these optimization problems and derive meaningful techno-economic insights for flexible consumers.

It studies the consumption decisions of a single flexible consumer in three steps of increasing complexity: a price-elastic hourly consumption (Question 1), the same decision under different models of the consumer's comfort preferences reflected in the objective function (Question 2), and finally an intertemporal requirement expressed as a constraint: a minimum daily energy consumption (Question 3). Questions 2 & 3 each change a single element compared to the previous question, so that the impact of every modeling choice can be isolated.

1. Price-elastic hourly consumption decisions of a flexible consumer

We consider a flexible residential consumer who owns the following assets:

  • A single flexible electrical load:
    • Hourly consumption can be completely adjusted between its minimum and maximum bounds (in KWh/h), Lmin and Lmax
    • The consumer is price-elastic: every kWh consumed in any hour is worth a constant marginal consumption utility uL (in DKK/kWh) to the consumer.
    • The consumer aims at maximizing its daily net utility (in DKK) - also called surplus
  • A rooftop photovoltaic (PV) system:
    • The hourly available PV production PVtmax (in KWh/h) for the following day is perfectly forecasted.
    • Producing one kWh of PV costs cPV (fixed marginal production cost; in DKK/kWh). IN the dataset provided, two different regimes of prices are considered: Case A with a cheap PV (cPV < uL) and Case B with an expensive PV (cPV > uL).

The consumer is connected to the distribution grid under the following techno-economic conditions:

  • It can import or export electricity to the grid, without any restriction.
  • It faces known, positive hourly electricity prices pt (in DKK/kWh) over a 24-hour horizon, at which electricity is bought from or sold to the retailer.
  • Fixed (positive) grid tariffs τimp and τexp are charged by the distribution system operator (DSO) on every unit imported from or exported to the grid (in DKK/kWh), respectively. The prosumer therefore does not face the market price pt directly, but the resulting effective import and export prices, ptimp and ptexp, which you will need to express in terms of  pt, τimp and τexp . We assume that the export grid tariff is always smaller than the hourly electricity prices pt
  1. Formulate the daily problem of this consumer: define the input data, decision variables, objective function and constraints, and state its properties: problem class, convexity, conditions on the input data under which it is feasible and bounded, and its size. Show that the daily problem decomposes into 24 independent hourly problems, write the hourly problem, and explain what this decomposition means computationally and economically.
  2. Formulate the Lagrange function, the dual problem, and the KKT conditions of the hourly problem. Does strong duality hold? Are the KKT conditions necessary and/or sufficient optimality conditions? Justify your answer from the properties stated in (a).
  3. In your own words, give the techno-economic interpretation of the dual variables associated with the hourly power balance, the PV availability limits, and the hourly load bounds. Express what the dual of the power balance is equal to in hours when the consumer is (i) importing, (ii) exporting, and (iii) when it neither imports nor exports and its load is strictly within its bounds.
  4. Price ladder. In this question, we only consider Case A (cPV < uL). Without numerically solving the hourly problem, use the strong duality and KKT conditions to derive the structure of its optimal solutions under different cost structures:
    1. Is it ever optimal for the consumer to import and export in the same hour? Demonstrate your answer. Which of the above-mentioned conditions on the price structure ensures this? Interpret this condition. 
    2. Load consumption. Assuming that Lmin < PVtmax < Lmax for every hour, identify three regimes for the optimal load in hour t, characterised by the relationship between uL, the effective import price, and the effective export price: one in which the optimal load is at its upper bound, one in which it is at its lower bound, and one in which it is between these bounds. State the conditions defining each regime, express the optimal value of the load in each regime, and interpret the three cases: what is the prosumer doing in each, and why does the optimum switch where it does?
    3. PV production. Still assuming that Lmin < PVtmax < Lmax for every hour, identify the regimes of curtailment and the thresholds on cPV that separate them. Interpret these conditions.
    4. Relax the previous assumption that Lmin < PVtmax < Lmax for every hour, and extend the analysis in ii and iii. to account for cases in which PVtmax < Lmin or PVtmax >Lmax. In which of the price regimes considered above does this change the optimal load consumption and PV curtailment? Express the new optimal solutions for these cases. 
    5. Summarize ii-iv as a decision rule: in each hour the consumer compares two given values, u and cPV, with the two effective import and export prices. What happens when one of these comparisons is a tie, and what does it imply for the uniqueness of the primal and dual solutions?
  5. Implement this optimization problem in a programming language of your choice, following the guidelines provided in the repository. Your code must return the primal solution, the objective value (and separately the procurement cost and the utility), and the dual variables of the constraints discussed in (c).
  6. Numerical validation. Solve the base case A. Present and analyze the main hourly results. Verify the price ladder of (d).ii–iii hour by hour. Solve the base case B. Present and analyze the main hourly results. Then explain the differences between the two cases and how the price ladder of (d).ii–iii is impacted. In both cases, analyze the values of dual variables (including any that is not uniquely determined).

2. Modeling the consumer's preferences in the objective function: separable disutility functions

We keep the parameters of Question 1 (case A) but change how the load preferences are represented in the objective. Instead of a constant marginal utility per kWh, the consumer has a preferred hourly consumption profile ℓreft (reference profile) and experiences a non-negative disutility when its consumption deviates from it, such that this disutility is minimal and equal to 0 when there is no deviation. All other costs and constraints remain the same. As in Question 1, every disutility considered is separable per hour.

    1. Consider a generic convex and differentiable disutility function. Adapt the problem of Question 1.(a): define all new notations (input parameters and decision variables), formulate the objective and constraints, and state how its properties (problem class, convexity, feasibility, decomposability per hour) compare with those of Question 1. Show that the objective of Question 1 corresponds to one particular choice of disutility function and reference profile: identify it, and note which of the assumptions above it relaxes.
    2. The disutility is now a linear function of the absolute deviation from the reference profile, with a linear coefficient cL > 0 in DKK/kWh. This function is not differentiable at 0.
        1. Write the corresponding hourly disutility function. Reformulate the hourly problem as a linear program by introducing appropriate continuous auxiliary variables and linear objective and constraints.
        2. Before solving, discuss qualitatively how the optimal load is expected to respond to cL, using a simple hourly cost-benefit argument (no duality is needed): For given effective import and export prices, do you expect the load to vary gradually when cL varies? Or to jump between a few candidate values? Do you expect the load to ever consume more than its reference profile, and why? You will verify these hypotheses in iv.
        3. Implement this model, following the guidelines of the repository.
        4. Solve the base case and present the optimal hourly schedule against the reference profile. Then vary cL over a range that spans the regimes you hypothesized in ii. Report for each run, the daily procurement cost, the total disutility, the daily energy consumed, the total absolute deviation, and the number of hours in which the deviation bound is binding. Identify from the sweep the values of cL at which the behaviour changes and relate them to the effective prices and marginal PV production cost. Do the results confirm your hypotheses in ii?
    3. The disutility is now a strictly convex quadratic function of the deviation with a quadratic coefficient cQ > 0 in DKK/kWh2 (and no linear coefficient cL=0).
        1. Derive the corresponding disutility function, and write the resulting hourly problem and state its problem class.
        2. Before solving, discuss qualitatively how the optimal load is expected to respond to cQ, using a simple hourly cost-benefit argument (no duality is needed): For given effective import and export prices, do you expect the load to vary gradually when cQ varies? Or to jump between a few candidate values? If so, express these values and thresholds. Do you expect the load to ever consume more than its reference profile, and why? You will verify these hypotheses in iv.
        3. Implement this model, following the guidelines of the repository.
        4. Solve the base case and present the optimal hourly schedule against the reference profile. Then repeat a similar sweep over cQ as in (b).iv for this model, with the same table of metrics. Do the results confirm your hypotheses of ii?
    4. Multi-model comparison. Using the base case and two or three scenarios selected from (b).iv and (c).iv, compare the linear and quadratic disutility models with each other. Analyze the metrics above and the shape of the resulting load profiles. Contrast the response to cQ in (c).iv with that observed in (b).iv for cL. In your own words, explain the impact of the objective functions: why does one model produce corner (bang-bang) solutions and the other a smooth, gradual response? Conclude with a recommendation: for which types of flexible loads and consumer preferences is each objective the more appropriate choice, and what does the choice imply for how a consumer would have to express its preferences?
    5. All the disutility functions above are separable in time: the discomfort caused by a deviation in hour t does not depend on what happens in the other hours. For many loads this is not realistic: a thermal load whose temperature drifts, or a consumer who accepts a short interruption but not a long one, suffers more from sustained deviations in consecutive hours than from the same total deviation spread over the day.
        1. Propose a disutility function that is not separable in time and has this property. Express it mathematically (one formulation suffices; several are possible), and state whether it is convex, how the problem class changes, and whether the problem still decomposes into hourly problems.
        2. Select adequate values for the cost parameters of this disutility function (base case) and implement this model, following the guidelines of the repository.

        3. Solve the base case and plot the optimal hourly schedule against the reference profile. Compare it with the schedules obtained in (b).iv and (c).iv: where do the deviations occur, how large are they, and how does the temporal coupling of your disutility function explain the difference?

        4. Investigate how the temporal structure of prices affects this consumer: what happens when prices alternate quickly between high and low from hour to hour, versus remaining high for several consecutive hours? Design a numerical experiment that isolates this effect, solve all the models of (c), (d) and (e), and compare the daily surplus and total deviations. Justify why your experiment allows a fair comparison — both across price profiles and across models.

3. Modeling the consumer's requirements in the constraints: a minimum daily energy consumption

We keep the consumer of Question 2.(c), with a quadratic disutility, and add a single new requirement: over the whole day, the consumer must consume at least a minimum total energy Emin (in kWh). Think of a load that must complete a daily duty whatever the prices: an electric vehicle that must be charged by the morning, a water heater, a batch process. This is the first constraint of the assignment that couples the hours of the day.

  1. Formulate the daily problem: starting from the model of Question 2.(c), define the new input data and the new constraint, and state how the properties of the problem change compared with Question 2: problem class, convexity, feasibility (state a condition on Emin, the reference profile, and the load bounds under which the problem is feasible), and whether the problem still decomposes into 24 independent hourly problems, and why.
  2. Formulate the Lagrange function, the dual problem and the KKT conditions of the daily problem, denoting by μ ≥ 0 the multiplier of the minimum-energy requirement. Does strong duality hold?
  3. Before solving — and without using the KKT conditions of (b) — discuss qualitatively the shape of the optimal solutions: Will the consumer ever consume above its hourly reference profile - why, and under which conditions? Will the consumer ever consume above its daily minimum requirement - why, and under which conditions? You will verify these hypotheses in (d) and (e).
  4. Implement this model, following the guidelines of the repository. 
  5. For the base case, present the optimal schedule against the reference profile and against the unconstrained consumer of Question 2.(c) on the same data, and check the hypotheses of (c) on the results.
  6. Sensitivity analysis. Design and run your own numerical experiments to reveal how the behaviour of this consumer changes across parameter values: you choose the most relevant parameters to vary (for instance Emin, the disutility coefficient, the hourly bounds, the level or the spread of the effective prices, etc - vary at least two of them, one at a time), over which ranges, and which results to present. Aim to reveal qualitatively different patterns of behaviour rather than to be exhaustive: a few well-chosen experiments, clearly presented and explained, will be rewarded over a large number of uncommented runs. Conclude on what determines when and how the minimum-energy requirement changes this consumer's behaviour.
  7. A small battery. The consumer now also owns a small battery, which it can charge from the grid or from its PV production and discharge to serve its load or to export. Its energy capacity (in kWh), charging and discharging power limits (in kW), charging and discharging efficiencies, and its initial state of charge are provided in the repository.
    1. Extend the formulation of (a): define the new input data and decision variables, and write the new constraints, including the state-of-charge dynamics that link consecutive hours. One modeling choice is deliberately left to you: how to treat the state of charge at the end of the horizon — in the constraints (for instance, requiring it to return to its initial level) or in the objective (for instance, valuing the energy left in the battery at the end of the day). Choose one representation, express it mathematically, and briefly justify your choice against at least one alternative: what behaviour do you expect each to produce in the last hours of the day?
    2. Your model will contain separate charging and discharging variables: discuss whether the two can be strictly positive in the same hour at an optimal solution. Argue why or why not for the data of this assignment (considering the efficiencies and price structure provided). State under which circumstances simultaneous charging and discharging would become a real risk, and how this could be fixed. No duality analysis or KKT conditions are required in this part.
    3. Implement this model, following the guidelines of the repository.
    4. Numerical analysis. Solve the base case with the battery (check in your results that charging and discharging never overlap, as argued in ii) and compare with (d): how does the battery change the optimal schedule, the deviations from the reference, the role of the minimum-energy requirement, and the net utility (value of the battery)?
    5. Sensitivity analysis. Design and run your own sensitivity analysis to investigate what impacts the value of the battery - defined as the difference between the consumer's total net utility with and without the battery, all else being equal. Select at least two relevant parameters to vary: one describing the environment the battery operates in (e.g. the hourly variation of prices over the day, the overall price level, or the temporal correlation between high prices and high net load) and at least one describing the battery itself (e.g. its capacity, power limits, or efficiencies) and conduct a sweep for each parameter (independently) with at least three values. Present only the results you find interesting, and explain in your own words why each parameter increases, decreases, or barely affects the value of the battery. Aim to reveal qualitatively different patterns of behaviour rather than to be exhaustive: a few well-chosen experiments, clearly presented and explained, will be rewarded over a large number of uncommented runs. Conclude: in what kind of environment is consumer-level storage worth the most, and which of its technical characteristics matter?
