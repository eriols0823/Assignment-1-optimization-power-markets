2Preparation: optimality guarantees and convexity

This section consolidates the preparation material (WLO 3.0–3.1). Before asking what a solver's answer is worth, recall where optimal solutions lie, how a solver reaches them, and why the class of the model matters.

Where do optimal solutions lie?

Linear programs
An optimal solution can always be found at a vertex of the feasible region. The candidates can be enumerated, the simplex method moves from vertex to vertex, solvers have guarantees when they converge, and optimality can be checked by hand.
Non-linear programs
The optimum may lie inside the feasible region, where the objective surface is flat, or on its boundary, where it is not. The candidates cannot be enumerated, solvers may converge without a global guarantee, and optimality cannot easily be checked by hand — unless the problem has more structure.

Some non-linear problems are much easier than others: those that are unconstrained (or whose optimum lies inside the feasible region) and those that are convex. The rest of this section says why.

Necessary and sufficient conditions

An optimality condition is a statement about \(x^*\) that you can check. Whether it screens candidates or certifies them depends on its direction:

Relation Meaning Logic
A is necessary for B B cannot hold unless A holds: if A fails, B fails. B ⇒ A
A is sufficient for B A holding is enough to conclude B. A ⇒ B
A is necessary and sufficient for B A and B hold together or fail together. A ⇔ B

The preparation slides use animals. Among the mammals, birds and insects shown, having a beak, laying eggs and having wings are all necessary for being a bird, but none is sufficient: the platypus has a beak and lays eggs; bats and bees have wings. “Can fly and has a beak” is sufficient in that group, but not necessary — the kiwi cannot fly. Having feathers is both. Optimality conditions follow the same logic: a necessary condition tells you where to look, a sufficient one tells you that you can stop looking.

Unconstrained problems: local optimality conditions

For \(\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^N} f(x)\) with \(f\) twice differentiable, observe a function and its derivatives: at a local minimum the function decreases before and increases after, so its slope is zero and still increasing. In several variables, the slope becomes the gradient and its rate of change the Hessian:

$$\nabla f(x^*) = \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}(x^*) \\ \vdots \\ \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_N}(x^*) \end{bmatrix},\qquad \nabla^2 f(x^*) = \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_1^2} & \cdots & \dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_1 \partial x_N} \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_N \partial x_1} & \cdots & \dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_N^2} \end{bmatrix}_{x = x^*}$$
Condition First derivative Second derivative Guarantees
Necessary \(\nabla f(x^*) = 0\) and \(\nabla^2 f(x^*) \succeq 0\) — all eigenvalues \(\ge 0\) If \(x^*\) is a local minimum, both hold.
Sufficient \(\nabla f(x^*) = 0\) and \(\nabla^2 f(x^*) \succ 0\) — all eigenvalues \(> 0\) If both hold, \(x^*\) is a (strict) local minimum.

Neither is both. The necessary conditions do not suffice: \(f(x) = x^3\) has \(f'(0) = 0\) and \(f''(0) = 0 \ge 0\), yet \(x = 0\) is not a minimum. The sufficient conditions are not necessary: \(f(x) = x^4\) has its minimum at \(0\), where \(f''(0) = 0\) is not \(> 0\).

These guarantees are only local

Both conditions involve derivatives — local information — so they can only compare \(x^*\) with its immediate neighbours. In general there are no necessary and sufficient global optimality conditions. Is there a class of “well-behaved” functions for which there are? That property is convexity: it turns any local optimum into a global one.

Convexity in three definitions

Definition 1 · Convex set
A set \(C \subseteq \mathbb{R}^N\) is convex iff \(\theta x + (1-\theta) y \in C\) for all \(x, y \in C\) and \(\theta \in [0,1]\).

In words: the straight segment between any two points of the set stays in the set. To disprove convexity, one pair of points whose segment leaves the set is enough.
Definition 2 · Convex function
A function \(f\) with convex domain is convex iff \(f(\theta x + (1-\theta) y)\) \(\le \theta f(x) + (1-\theta) f(y)\) for all \(x, y\) and \(\theta \in [0,1]\).

In words: the chord joining two points of the graph never dips below it; equivalently, the epigraph \(\{(x,t): f(x) \le t\}\) is a convex set. \(f\) is concave iff \(-f\) is convex; linear functions are both.
Definition 3 · Convex problem
A problem in standard form is convex when (1) the objective \(f\) is convex, (2) every inequality function \(g_j\) is convex, and (3) every equality function \(h_i\) is affine, \(h_i(x) = a_i^\top x - b_i\).

Maximising a concave objective — such as social welfare — is the same problem as minimising its negative.
Convex functions, if differentiable

A differentiable \(f\) with convex domain is convex iff

$$f(y) \ge f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top (y - x) \qquad \forall x, y \in \mathrm{dom}(f).$$

The graph lies above every tangent (hyper)plane: the first-order Taylor approximation is a global under-estimator of \(f\). If \(f\) is twice differentiable, this is equivalent to \(\nabla^2 f(x) \succeq 0\) everywhere — for instance, a quadratic cost \(a p^2 + b p\) is convex exactly when \(a \ge 0\).

Convex optimization problem $$\begin{aligned} \min_{x \in \mathbb{R}^N}\ & f(x) && \text{convex} \\ \text{s.t. }\ & h_i(x) = 0, \quad \forall i = 1,\dots,I && \text{affine} \\ & g_j(x) \le 0, \quad \forall j = 1,\dots,J && \text{convex} \end{aligned}$$
Notation — indices swap relative to Week 1

The Week 1 standard form (and the preparation slides) index equalities \(h_j\), \(j = 1,\dots,m_e\), and inequalities \(g_i\), \(i = 1,\dots,m_i\). From here on these notes follow the lecture slides: equalities \(h_i\), \(i = 1,\dots,I\), with multipliers \(\lambda_i\); inequalities \(g_j\), \(j = 1,\dots,J\), with multipliers \(\mu_j\).

Why equalities must be affine, while inequalities need only be convex. A constraint \(g_j(x) \le 0\) with convex \(g_j\) keeps a sublevel set of a convex function, and such sets are convex. A constraint \(h_i(x) = 0\) keeps a surface, and a surface is in general convex only if it is flat. Asking for \(h_i\) affine guarantees it.

The ideal case: unconstrained convex problems

In a convex optimization problem, every local optimum is a global optimum.

The property that makes “optimal” mean optimal.

The argument, in one step. Let \(x^*\) be a local optimum and suppose some feasible \(y\) had \(f(y) < f(x^*)\). Every point \(z = \theta y + (1-\theta) x^*\) is feasible, because the feasible set is convex, and by convexity of \(f\), \(f(z) \le \theta f(y) + (1-\theta) f(x^*) < f(x^*)\) for every \(\theta \in (0,1]\) — including \(\theta\) arbitrarily small. Points arbitrarily close to \(x^*\) would then improve on it, so \(x^*\) was not a local optimum after all.

Necessary and sufficient global condition $$\min_{x \in \mathbb{R}^N} f(x), \ \ f \text{ convex:} \qquad \hat x \text{ is a global minimum} \iff \nabla f(\hat x) = 0$$

No second-order condition is needed: for a convex, twice-differentiable \(f\), \(\nabla^2 f \succeq 0\) holds everywhere, and the tangent-plane inequality above with \(\nabla f(\hat x) = 0\) gives \(f(y) \ge f(\hat x)\) for every \(y\).

When you read a solver log: if the problem is not convex, “optimal” means the solver stopped — last week's AC-OPF made no claim about anything it did not visit. If the problem is convex, nothing better exists anywhere.

The DC and AC flow equations of Week 2 are both equality constraints, and both are perfectly smooth functions. Read as sets of feasible (angle difference, flow) pairs, only one of them is convex:

Why Week 2 was safe

Proving convexity of a model — a checklist
  • Variables: all continuous? A single binary or integer variable makes the feasible set non-convex.
  • Objective: linear, or convex for a minimisation (concave for a maximisation)? Check the Hessian if in doubt.
  • Every inequality written as \(g_j(x) \le 0\): is each \(g_j\) convex? Affine is fine.
  • Every equality written as \(h_i(x) = 0\): is each \(h_i\) affine?

If every answer is yes, the problem is convex. The rule is sufficient, not necessary: a failed check means convexity is not guaranteed, whereas a counterexample — two feasible points whose midpoint is infeasible, as in the AC figure — proves that the feasible set is not convex.

Can we generalise to constrained problems?

Not directly. When the optimum lies on the boundary of the feasible region, the objective still wants to decrease — \(\nabla f(x^*) \ne 0\) — but every improving step leaves the feasible set: the constraint is holding \(x^*\) in place. The condition \(\nabla f(x^*) = 0\) is simply false there, yet \(x^*\) is optimal.

What we are missing to tie the three loose ends
  • A certificate of whether a solution is optimal, without enumerating candidates (Q1).
  • Optimality conditions that account for the constraints along the boundary of the feasible region (Q2).
  • A way to measure and explain how much each constraint affects the solution (Q3).

3The Lagrangian: remove the constraints, charge for them

Goal: derive necessary and sufficient global optimality conditions for constrained convex problems. The idea is to turn the constrained problem back into an equivalent unconstrained one, where “gradient = 0” applies again. We start with inequality constraints only; equalities follow in section 4.

Primal problem (constrained) $$p^* = \min_{x \in \mathbb{R}^N}\ f(x) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad g_j(x) \le 0, \quad \forall j = 1,\dots,J$$

Step 1 — Penalise the constraints with an infinite step

Move each constraint into the objective, with a penalty that is zero when the constraint is satisfied and infinite when it is violated:

$$I\big[g_j(x)\big] = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } g_j(x) \le 0 \\ +\infty & \text{if } g_j(x) > 0 \end{cases}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad p^* = \min_{x \in \mathbb{R}^N}\ f(x) + \sum_{j=1}^{J} I\big[g_j(x)\big]$$

The minimisation is now unconstrained and returns exactly \(p^*\), since any infeasible \(x\) costs \(+\infty\). But the penalised objective jumps to infinity at the boundary: it is not a function we can work with.

Step 2 — Replace the step by linear under-estimators

For any \(\mu \ge 0\), the line \(\mu\, g(x)\) lies below the step everywhere: for \(g(x) \le 0\), \(\mu g(x) \le 0 = I[g(x)]\); for \(g(x) > 0\), \(\mu g(x)\) is finite. It penalises violation and rewards slack. Taking the largest of these lines recovers the step exactly:

The infinite step \(I[g]\) (red) and the lines \(\mu\, g\) for several \(\mu \ge 0\) (blue). Each line under-estimates the step; their upper envelope is the step itself.

$$\mu\, g(x) \le I\big[g(x)\big] \quad \forall \mu \ge 0, \qquad\qquad \max_{\mu \ge 0}\ \mu\, g(x) = I\big[g(x)\big]$$

Step 3 — The Lagrangian and the primal Lagrange problem

The function being maximised is the Lagrangian (Lagrange function), with one multiplier (dual variable) \(\mu_j \ge 0\) per constraint:

$$\mathcal{L}(x, \mu) = f(x) + \sum_{j=1}^{J} \mu_j\, g_j(x) = f(x) + \mu^\top g(x), \qquad \mu \ge 0$$

For each fixed \(x\), maximising over \(\mu\) gives back the penalised objective, and minimising over \(x\) gives back the primal problem — the primal Lagrange problem:

$$\phi(x) = \max_{\mu \ge 0}\ \mathcal{L}(x, \mu) = \begin{cases} f(x) & \text{if } g(x) \le 0 \\ +\infty & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad p^* = \min_{x \in \mathbb{R}^N}\ \phi(x) = \min_{x \in \mathbb{R}^N}\ \max_{\mu \ge 0}\ \mathcal{L}(x, \mu)$$
The primal Lagrange problem is… Because…
✓ unconstrained The constraints have moved into the objective.
✓ convex in x (for a convex primal) \(\phi\) is a pointwise maximum of the convex functions \(f + \mu^\top g\), \(\mu \ge 0\).
✗ hard to use for optimality conditions \(\phi\) has no explicit, differentiable expression: it is the infinite step in disguise.

Read the Lagrangian as a charging rule: each constraint is removed from the feasible set and charged for in the objective, at a rate \(\mu_j\) per unit of \(g_j(x)\). For any \(\mu \ge 0\) and any feasible \(x\), the charge is non-positive, so the Lagrangian never exceeds the true cost on the feasible set:

$$\mathcal{L}(x, \mu) = f(x) + \underbrace{\sum_{j} \mu_j\, g_j(x)}_{\le\, 0} \ \le\ f(x) \qquad \forall x \text{ feasible},\ \forall \mu \ge 0$$

Swapping min and max: a two-player game

The min–max form reads as a sequential game between the X player, who chooses \(x\) to minimise \(\mathcal{L}\), and the U player, who chooses the multipliers \(\mu \ge 0\) to maximise it. What happens if the order of the two operators is inverted?

Primal Lagrange problem
\(\displaystyle p^* = \min_{x} \max_{\mu \ge 0} \mathcal{L}(x,\mu)\)

The X player moves first; the U player reacts, maximising \(\mathcal{L}\) over \(\mu\) for the fixed \(x\). Any violated constraint is met with \(\mu_j \to \infty\), so the X player is forced to stay feasible.
Dual Lagrange problem
\(\displaystyle d^* = \max_{\mu \ge 0} \min_{x} \mathcal{L}(x,\mu)\)

The U player moves first and commits to multipliers; the X player reacts, minimising \(\mathcal{L}\) over \(x\) for the fixed \(\mu\).

Which player would you rather be? In a sequential zero-sum game the player who moves second has the advantage: it sees the other's choice before making its own. In the dual problem that is the minimising X player, so the dual value can only be lower — section 4. The dual is also a much simpler problem: for any given \(\mu \ge 0\), the X player faces a convex (for a convex primal) and unconstrained problem, whose solution can be written explicitly through its first-order condition \(\nabla_x \mathcal{L} = 0\) and plugged into the U player's problem.

Working example

The running example of the lecture videos minimises a linear function over the unit disc:

$$\min_{x \in \mathbb{R}^2}\ x_1 + x_2 \quad \text{s.t.} \quad g(x) = x_1^2 + x_2^2 - 1 \le 0\qquad\longrightarrow\qquad \mathcal{L}(x,\mu) = x_1 + x_2 + \mu\,(x_1^2 + x_2^2 - 1)$$

The objective is linear and the constraint function is convex, so the problem is convex. The point \(x = (0,0)\) satisfies the constraint strictly, which will matter in section 4.

These notes write the multiplier as \(\mu\) and keep \(u\) for the perturbation only.
  • The weak- and strong-duality figures draw the dual objective as \(g(\lambda)\), with multiplier \(\lambda\). Here the dual function is \(q(\mu)\) — or \(q(\lambda, \mu)\) with equality constraints — to avoid a clash with the constraint functions \(g_j\).

4Q1 — Lagrange duality: the best lower bound

Q1 — can we guarantee anything about the optimality of a solution? Swapping the order of the two players gives the dual problem. Its value is always a lower bound on the primal optimum; under convexity and a mild regularity condition, it equals the optimum.

The dual function and the dual problem

Fix the multipliers and let the X player respond. The result is the dual (Lagrange) function:

$$q(\mu) = \min_{x \in \mathbb{R}^N}\ \mathcal{L}(x, \mu) = \min_{x \in \mathbb{R}^N} \big[ f(x) + \mu^\top g(x) \big],\qquad d^* = \max_{\mu \ge 0}\ q(\mu) = \max_{\mu \ge 0}\ \min_{x \in \mathbb{R}^N}\ \mathcal{L}(x, \mu)$$

Strictly, the minimum is an infimum and may equal \(-\infty\): for some multipliers the X player can push the Lagrangian down without limit.

Because \(\mathcal{L}\) is affine in \(\mu\) for each fixed \(x\), and a pointwise minimum of affine functions is concave, \(q\) is always concave. The dual problem — maximising a concave function over \(\mu \ge 0\) — is therefore always a convex problem, even when the primal is not.

Weak duality

Why the dual can only be lower. For any \(\bar x\) and any \(\bar\mu \ge 0\),

$$q(\bar\mu) = \min_{x} \mathcal{L}(x, \bar\mu)\ \le\ \mathcal{L}(\bar x, \bar\mu)\ \le\ \max_{\mu \ge 0} \mathcal{L}(\bar x, \mu) = \phi(\bar x)$$

Every value of the dual function lies below every value of \(\phi\). Maximising the left-hand side over \(\bar\mu\) and minimising the right-hand side over \(\bar x\) keeps the order:

Weak duality — always true $$d^* = \max_{\mu \ge 0} \min_{x} \mathcal{L}(x,\mu)\ \le\ \min_{x} \max_{\mu \ge 0} \mathcal{L}(x,\mu) = p^*$$

The dual objective is an under-estimator of the primal objective: every admissible choice of multipliers gives a valid lower bound on the optimal value, and the dual optimum is the best global lower bound. This holds for every problem — convex or not, linear or not.

The difference \(p^* - d^* \ge 0\) is the duality gap. Weak duality gives a certificate you can compute while solving: for any feasible \(\tilde x\) and any dual-feasible \(\tilde\mu\),

$$0 \ \le\ f(\tilde x) - p^* \ \le\ f(\tilde x) - q(\tilde\mu)$$

so the distance between a candidate's cost and any dual value is an upper limit on how far from optimal the candidate is. Without further properties of the primal problem, however, the bound is not guaranteed to be tight (the gap may be positive), and the dual cannot replace the primal for deriving optimality conditions.

Strong duality

In some cases the gap is zero, \(p^* = d^*\): strong duality holds. The best bound equals the optimal value, the order of the players no longer matters, and the dual can replace the primal when deriving optimality conditions — the route taken in section 6.

Optimality certificate

If \(\tilde x\) is primal feasible, \(\tilde\mu \ge 0\), and \(f(\tilde x) = q(\tilde\mu)\), then \(\tilde x\) is primal optimal and \(\tilde\mu\) is dual optimal — because \(f(\tilde x) = q(\tilde\mu) \le d^* \le p^* \le f(\tilde x)\).

Under what conditions does strong duality hold? Convexity, together with a mild constraint qualification:

Slater's condition (weak form)

If the primal problem is convex and there is a point \(\hat x\) with \(h_i(\hat x) = 0\) for every equality, \(g_j(\hat x) < 0\) for every non-linear inequality, and \(g_j(\hat x) \le 0\) for every affine inequality, then strong duality holds: \(p^* = d^*\).

This condition is sufficient but not necessary: strong duality can hold without it.

When all constraints are affine, Slater's condition reduces to feasibility. The ED, ED with ATC and DC-OPF are convex with only linear constraints, so strong duality holds whenever they are feasible. The AC-OPF is not convex, so nothing guarantees a matching bound — loose end 1, seen from the dual side.

Dealing with equality constraints

An equality is two inequalities: \(h_i(x) = 0 \iff h_i(x) \le 0\ (: \mu_i^+ \ge 0)\) and \(-h_i(x) \le 0\ (: \mu_i^- \ge 0)\). The same recipe gives two penalty terms per equality:

$$\mathcal{L} = f(x) + \mu^\top g(x) + \mu^{+\top} h(x) - \mu^{-\top} h(x) = f(x) + \mu^\top g(x) + \big(\mu^+ - \mu^-\big)^\top h(x)$$

The pair \(\mu_i^+, \mu_i^-\) only ever appears through its difference, which can take any sign. Replacing it by \(\lambda_i = \mu_i^+ - \mu_i^- \in \mathbb{R}\):

Inequality constraints only
\(\mathcal{L}(x,\mu) = f(x) + \mu^\top g(x)\)
\(\displaystyle d^* = \max_{\mu \ge 0} \min_{x} \mathcal{L}(x,\mu)\)

One multiplier \(\mu_j \ge 0\) per inequality: a violation (\(g_j > 0\)) is penalised, never rewarded.
With equality constraints
\(\mathcal{L}(x,\lambda,\mu) = f(x) + \lambda^\top h(x) + \mu^\top g(x)\)
\(\displaystyle d^* = \max_{\lambda \in \mathbb{R}^I,\, \mu \ge 0} \min_{x} \mathcal{L}(x,\lambda,\mu)\)

\(\lambda\) free, \(\mu \ge 0\): an equality can be violated in both directions, so its multiplier can take either sign.
The recipe — one dual variable per constraint
  • Write every equality as \(h_i(x) = 0\) and attach \(\lambda_i\), free in sign.
  • Write every inequality as \(g_j(x) \le 0\) and attach \(\mu_j \ge 0\).
  • Add \(\sum_i \lambda_i h_i(x) + \sum_j \mu_j g_j(x)\) to the objective — each term is multiplier × (lhs − rhs) of its constraint in this form.

The sign of a multiplier depends on how its constraint was written. Fix the convention first — here always “= 0” and “≤ 0” — and read every sign off the Lagrangian.

Working example, continued

For \(\mu > 0\), \(\mathcal{L}(x,\mu) = x_1 + x_2 + \mu(x_1^2 + x_2^2 - 1)\) is a convex quadratic in \(x\). The X player's first-order condition gives \(1 + 2\mu x_1 = 0\) and \(1 + 2\mu x_2 = 0\), so \(x_1 = x_2 = -1/(2\mu)\). Plugging this into the U player's problem:

$$q(\mu) = -\frac{1}{2\mu} - \frac{1}{2\mu} + \mu\Big(\frac{2}{4\mu^2} - 1\Big) = -\mu - \frac{1}{2\mu}\quad (\mu > 0), \qquad q(0) = \min_x\ (x_1 + x_2) = -\infty$$

Maximising, \(q'(\mu) = -1 + 1/(2\mu^2) = 0\) gives \(\mu^* = 1/\sqrt{2}\) and \(d^* = -\sqrt{2}\). On the primal side, the point of the disc furthest in the direction \(-(1,1)\) is \(x^* = (-1/\sqrt{2}, -1/\sqrt{2})\), with \(p^* = -\sqrt{2}\). The gap is zero, as Slater's condition predicts: the problem is convex and \(\hat x = (0,0)\) is strictly feasible.

Dual function of the working example. Every \(\mu \ge 0\) gives a lower bound on \(p^*\); the best bound, at \(\mu^* = 1/\sqrt{2}\), reaches it.

When strong duality fails

Without convexity the gap can be strictly positive. Consider

$$\min_{x \in \mathbb{R}}\ -\frac{1}{1+x^2} \quad \text{s.t.} \quad 1 - x^2 \le 0$$

The feasible set \(\{x : |x| \ge 1\}\) is not convex. The primal optimum is at \(x = \pm 1\), with \(p^* = -1/2\). On the dual side, \(\mathcal{L}(x,\mu) = -1/(1+x^2) + \mu(1 - x^2)\): for any \(\mu > 0\) it tends to \(-\infty\) as \(|x|\) grows, so \(q(\mu) = -\infty\); for \(\mu = 0\), \(q(0) = \min_x -1/(1+x^2) = -1\), attained at \(x = 0\). Hence \(d^* = -1 < p^* = -1/2\): the best bound is valid, but it never confirms the optimum.

The duality process at a glance

  1. Primal problem

    \(p^* = \min_x f(x)\) s.t. \(g_j(x) \le 0\) for all \(j\) — constrained.

  2. Penalise and relax

    Replace the infinite step by linear under-estimators: \(\phi(x) = \max_{\mu \ge 0} \mathcal{L}(x,\mu)\).

  3. Primal Lagrange problem

    \(p^* = \min_x \max_{\mu \ge 0} \mathcal{L}(x,\mu)\) — equivalent, unconstrained, but hard to analyse.

  4. Dual Lagrange problem

    Swap the players: \(d^* = \max_{\mu \ge 0} \min_x \mathcal{L}(x,\mu) \le p^*\) — always a lower bound, and equal to \(p^*\) if strong duality holds.

5LP duality: a special case of Lagrange duality

Same construction as before — now on a problem whose objective and constraints are all linear. Linear programs are convex, their constraints are affine, and their Lagrangian can be minimised by hand. Applying the steps of sections 3 and 4 to an LP returns another LP: the LP dual. We do it first on the economic dispatch, then in general, and then show that the classic “direct” construction gives the same result.

The economic dispatch and its toy instance

The ED of Week 2 matches production and consumption at the greatest total value. With inflexible loads, the utility of consumption is a constant and the objective reduces to minimising production cost:

$$\begin{aligned} \min_{p}\ & \sum_{g=1}^{G} c_g\, p_g \\ \text{s.t. }\ & \sum_{g=1}^{G} p_g = L \\ & 0 \le p_g \le \overline{P}_g, \qquad \forall g = 1,\dots,G \end{aligned}$$

\(G\): number of committed generators; \(p_g\): dispatch of generator \(g\) (decision variable); \(c_g\): marginal production cost; \(\overline{P}_g\): capacity; \(L\): inflexible load. The superscript \(G\) of Week 2 (\(c^G_g\), \(p^G_g\), \(\overline{P}^G_g\)) is dropped in this section for readability.

The toy instance has three committed generators and one inflexible load, with no network constraints:

G1 G2 G3 L1
Marginal cost \(c_g\) (DKK/kWh) 70 15 150 —
Capacity \(\overline{P}_g\) (kW) 150 150 150 —
Load (kW) — — — 200
Optimal dispatch (kWh/h) 50 150 0 200

The merit order loads G2 to capacity and G1 with the remaining 50 kW, at a cost \(z^* = 70 \cdot 50 + 15 \cdot 150 = 5750\) DKK. Is that optimal? The dual will prove it.

Step 1 — Standard form, one multiplier per constraint

$$\begin{aligned} \min_{p}\ & \sum_{g=1}^{G} c_g\, p_g \\ \text{s.t. }\ & L - \sum_{g=1}^{G} p_g = 0 && : \lambda \in \mathbb{R} \\ & {-p_g} \le 0, \quad \forall g && : \underline{\mu}_g \ge 0 \\ & p_g - \overline{P}_g \le 0, \quad \forall g && : \overline{\mu}_g \ge 0 \end{aligned}$$

\(\lambda\): multiplier of the power balance; \(\underline{\mu}_g\), \(\overline{\mu}_g\): multipliers of the lower and upper bounds of generator \(g\). In the toy instance all are in DKK/kWh — the unit of the objective divided by the unit of the constraint.

Step 2 — The Lagrangian

$$\mathcal{L}(p, \lambda, \underline{\mu}, \overline{\mu}) = \sum_{g} c_g\, p_g + \lambda\Big(L - \sum_{g} p_g\Big) - \sum_{g} \underline{\mu}_g\, p_g + \sum_{g} \overline{\mu}_g\,\big(p_g - \overline{P}_g\big)$$

Regroup by decision variable: every term that multiplies \(p_g\), plus a constant.

$$\mathcal{L} = \underbrace{\lambda L - \sum_{g} \overline{\mu}_g\, \overline{P}_g}_{\text{constant in } p} + \sum_{g} \underbrace{\big(c_g - \lambda - \underline{\mu}_g + \overline{\mu}_g\big)}_{\text{coefficient fixed by the multipliers}}\, p_g$$

Step 3 — Minimise over p: the dual function

All bounds have moved into the objective, so \(p\) is free in \(\mathbb{R}^G\). A linear function of a free variable is unbounded below unless its coefficient is exactly zero:

$$q(\lambda, \underline{\mu}, \overline{\mu}) = \min_{p \in \mathbb{R}^G} \mathcal{L} = \begin{cases} \lambda L - \sum_{g} \overline{\mu}_g\, \overline{P}_g & \text{if } c_g - \lambda - \underline{\mu}_g + \overline{\mu}_g = 0, \quad \forall g \\ -\infty & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

Multipliers that violate the coefficient condition give the useless bound \(-\infty\); all others give a finite lower bound on \(z^*\).

Step 4 — Maximise the dual function: another LP

The best bound excludes the −∞ branch by writing the coefficient condition as a constraint:

$$\begin{aligned} \max_{\lambda,\ \underline{\mu} \ge 0,\ \overline{\mu} \ge 0}\ & \lambda L - \sum_{g=1}^{G} \overline{\mu}_g\, \overline{P}_g \\ \text{s.t. }\ & \lambda + \underline{\mu}_g - \overline{\mu}_g = c_g, \qquad \forall g \end{aligned}$$

Each \(\underline{\mu}_g\) appears only in its own constraint, as a non-negative slack. Eliminating it turns each equality into an inequality:

Dual of the economic dispatch $$\max_{\lambda,\ \overline{\mu} \ge 0}\ \lambda L - \sum_{g=1}^{G} \overline{\mu}_g\, \overline{P}_g \qquad \text{s.t.} \qquad \lambda - \overline{\mu}_g \le c_g, \quad \forall g$$

This is again a linear program — linear objective in the multipliers, linear constraints — with one dual variable per primal constraint and one dual constraint per primal variable.

G2 \(70 - 55 \le 15\) 0 \(p_2^* = 150\): at capacity
G3 \(70 - 0 \le 150\) 80 \(p_3^* = 0\): at its lower bound

With \(\lambda^* = 70\) and \(\overline{\mu}^* = (0, 55, 0)\), the dual objective is \(70 \cdot 200 - 55 \cdot 150 = 5750 = z^*\). A feasible dispatch and feasible multipliers with equal objective values: by the certificate of section 4, both are optimal. The merit-order dispatch is proven optimal, and the gap is zero — strong duality.

The general case: two routes to the LP dual

The same steps apply to any LP. Take the standard LP form — non-negative variables, “≥” constraints, \(A \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times N}\) with one row per constraint and one column per variable — and attach one multiplier \(y_j \ge 0\) per constraint. The only choice left is what to do with \(x \ge 0\).

Route A

Treat \(x \ge 0\) as constraints

Write \(b - Ax \le 0\ (: y \ge 0)\) and \(-x \le 0\ (: \mu \ge 0)\), so that \(x\) is free:$$\begin{aligned}\mathcal{L}(x,y,\mu) &= c^\top x + y^\top (b - Ax) - \mu^\top x \\ &= y^\top b + (c - A^\top y - \mu)^\top x\end{aligned}$$$$q(y,\mu) = \begin{cases} y^\top b & \text{if } A^\top y + \mu = c \\ -\infty & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$The dual has an equality constraint in which \(\mu \ge 0\) only appears as a slack. Eliminating it turns the equality into \(A^\top y \le c\).
Route B

Keep \(x \ge 0\) in the inner minimisation

Attach only \(y \ge 0\) to \(Ax \ge b\) and minimise over \(x \ge 0\):$$\mathcal{L}(x,y) = c^\top x + y^\top (b - Ax) = y^\top b + (c - A^\top y)^\top x$$$$q(y) = y^\top b + \inf_{x \ge 0}\, (c - A^\top y)^\top x = \begin{cases} y^\top b & \text{if } A^\top y \le c \\ -\infty & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$A linear function over \(x \ge 0\) is bounded below only if every coefficient is \(\ge 0\) — and then its infimum is 0, at \(x = 0\).
Two routes, one dual: the standard LP pair $$\min_{x \ge 0}\ c^\top x \ \ \text{s.t.}\ \ Ax \ge b \qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad \max_{y \ge 0}\ b^\top y \ \ \text{s.t.}\ \ A^\top y \le c$$

Why “\(\le c\)” and not “\(= c\)”? Because the primal variables are non-negative: the coefficient of each \(x_i\) only needs to be non-negative for the inner minimisation to be bounded. The direct approach below enforces the same rule in words: the coefficient of each \(x_i\) in a combination of constraints must not exceed its cost \(c_i\).

Primal–dual standard formulation

Primal linear problem (P)
$$\begin{aligned} \min_{x}\ & z = \textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{N} c_i x_i \\ \text{s.t. }\ & \textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{N} A_{ji} x_i \ge b_j, \ \ \forall j \\ & x_i \ge 0, \ \ \forall i \end{aligned}$$In matrix form: \(\min c^\top x\) s.t. \(Ax \ge b\), \(x \ge 0\).
Dual linear problem (D)
$$\begin{aligned} \max_{y}\ & \tilde z = \textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{M} b_j y_j \\ \text{s.t. }\ & \textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{M} A_{ji} y_j \le c_i, \ \ \forall i \\ & y_j \ge 0, \ \ \forall j \end{aligned}$$In matrix form: \(\max b^\top y\) s.t. \(A^\top y \le c\), \(y \ge 0\).

\(x = [x_1, \dots, x_N]^\top\) and \(y = [y_1, \dots, y_M]^\top\) are the primal and dual variables, \(c\) the cost vector, \(b\) the right-hand-side vector, and \(A\) the constraint matrix. There is one dual constraint per primal variable and one dual variable per primal constraint.

Watch the inequality directions

The symmetric pairs are: a minimisation with “\(\ge\)” constraints and a maximisation with “\(\le\)” constraints — \(\min c^\top x\) s.t. \(Ax \ge b\), \(x \ge 0\) \(\leftrightarrow\) \(\max b^\top y\) s.t. \(A^\top y \le c\), \(y \ge 0\) — and, read the other way, \(\max c^\top x\) s.t. \(Ax \le b\), \(x \ge 0\) \(\leftrightarrow\) \(\min b^\top y\) s.t. \(A^\top y \ge c\), \(y \ge 0\). Mixing the two (a minimisation with \(Ax \le b\) and a dual with \(A^\top y \ge c\), \(y \ge 0\)) is not a valid pair; when in doubt, derive the dual through the Lagrangian.

Notation — index order of the constraint matrix

These notes write the coefficient of variable \(i\) in constraint \(j\) as \(A_{ji}\), so that \(A \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times N}\) and \(Ax \ge b\) is well defined, as in Week 1. If a slide displays \(A\) with \(N\) rows and \(M\) columns, read it as the transpose of the matrix used here.

The direct approach: the same dual, built by hand

LP duality is often introduced without the Lagrangian, by asking how to convince someone — a market participant, say — that a dispatch is optimal. Any feasible dispatch gives an upper bound on \(z^*\). If you can also derive a valid lower bound with the same value, the dispatch is optimal. Lower bounds can be built from the constraints themselves.

Write every constraint of the toy model in “≥” form and give it a non-negative weight:

$$\begin{aligned} \min_{p \ge 0}\ & 70 p_1 + 15 p_2 + 150 p_3 && (\Omega) \\ \text{s.t. }\ & p_1 + p_2 + p_3 \ge 200 && (\Gamma_{1a}) : \alpha_{1a} \ge 0 \\ & {-p_1} - p_2 - p_3 \ge -200 && (\Gamma_{1b}) : \alpha_{1b} \ge 0 \\ & p_1 \ge 0, \qquad -p_1 \ge -150 && (\Gamma_{2a}, \Gamma_{2b}) : \alpha_{2a}, \alpha_{2b} \ge 0 \\ & p_2 \ge 0, \qquad -p_2 \ge -150 && (\Gamma_{3a}, \Gamma_{3b}) : \alpha_{3a}, \alpha_{3b} \ge 0 \\ & p_3 \ge 0, \qquad -p_3 \ge -150 && (\Gamma_{4a}, \Gamma_{4b}) : \alpha_{4a}, \alpha_{4b} \ge 0 \end{aligned}$$

Adding the weighted constraints gives a new valid inequality:

$$\begin{aligned} &\big(\alpha_{1a} - \alpha_{1b} + \alpha_{2a} - \alpha_{2b}\big)\, p_1 + \big(\alpha_{1a} - \alpha_{1b} + \alpha_{3a} - \alpha_{3b}\big)\, p_2 + \big(\alpha_{1a} - \alpha_{1b} + \alpha_{4a} - \alpha_{4b}\big)\, p_3 \\ &\qquad \ge\ 200\,(\alpha_{1a} - \alpha_{1b}) - 150\,(\alpha_{2b} + \alpha_{3b} + \alpha_{4b}) \end{aligned}$$

Since \(p \ge 0\), if each coefficient on the left is no larger than the corresponding cost in \((\Omega)\), then \((\Omega) \ge\) left-hand side \(\ge\) right-hand side for every feasible \(p\): the right-hand side is a valid lower bound on \(z^*\). A few trials, with all unlisted weights at zero:

Trial Non-zero weights Coefficients (G1, G2, G3) Lower bound (DKK)
1 \(\alpha_{1b} = 15\) −15, −15, −15 −3000
2 \(\alpha_{1a} = 15\) 15, 15, 15 3000
3 \(\alpha_{1a} = 150,\ \alpha_{2b} = 80,\ \alpha_{3b} = 135\) 70, 15, 150 −2250
4 \(\alpha_{1a} = 70,\ \alpha_{3b} = 55\) 70, 15, 70 5750

Trial 4 reaches 5750, the cost of the merit-order dispatch, so that dispatch is optimal. Trial 3 shows that copying the objective coefficients exactly is not the goal — only the inequality matters. Finding the best weights is itself an optimization problem: the LP dual.

Dual built by hand $$\begin{aligned} \max_{\alpha \ge 0}\ & 200\,(\alpha_{1a} - \alpha_{1b}) - 150\,(\alpha_{2b} + \alpha_{3b} + \alpha_{4b}) \\ \text{s.t. }\ & \alpha_{1a} - \alpha_{1b} + \alpha_{2a} - \alpha_{2b} \le 70 \\ & \alpha_{1a} - \alpha_{1b} + \alpha_{3a} - \alpha_{3b} \le 15 \\ & \alpha_{1a} - \alpha_{1b} + \alpha_{4a} - \alpha_{4b} \le 150 \end{aligned}$$

LP duality is Lagrange duality

The direct approach is the dual function written out term by term, with the inner minimisation solved by hand. The coefficient condition \(A^\top y \le c\) is what makes a combination of constraints a valid lower bound — and it is exactly the condition under which \(q\) is finite. For LPs, use whichever derivation you find easier; the result is the same.

Non-standard LPs

Most LPs are not in standard form: they have free or non-positive variables, equality constraints, or inequalities in the other direction. The Lagrange route needs no special rules — the signs come out of the Lagrangian:

  • An equality constraint gets a free multiplier.
  • A free variable has no sign to fall back on, so its coefficient in the Lagrangian must vanish: its dual constraint is an equality.
  • A non-negative variable is handled as \(-x_i \le 0\) with its own multiplier; eliminating that multiplier turns its dual constraint into an inequality.

For example, with equality constraints, inequality constraints and free variables — writing the constraints as \(b_{eq} - A_{eq}x = 0\) and \(b_{ineq} - A_{ineq}x \le 0\):

Primal and its Lagrangian $$\begin{aligned} & \min_{x \in \mathbb{R}^N}\ c^\top x \quad \text{s.t.} \quad A_{eq} x = b_{eq}\ \ (:\lambda), \qquad A_{ineq} x \ge b_{ineq}\ \ (:\mu \ge 0) \\ & \mathcal{L} = b_{eq}^\top \lambda + b_{ineq}^\top \mu + \big(c - A_{eq}^\top \lambda - A_{ineq}^\top \mu\big)^\top x \end{aligned}$$
Dual $$\max_{\lambda \text{ free},\ \mu \ge 0}\ b_{eq}^\top \lambda + b_{ineq}^\top \mu \quad \text{s.t.} \quad A_{eq}^\top \lambda + A_{ineq}^\top \mu = c$$

Writing the equality the other way round, \(A_{eq}x - b_{eq} = 0\), only flips the sign of the free multiplier \(\lambda\).

Two alternatives are common in textbooks. Both are useful as a cross-check:

Step-by-step transformation
Rewrite the LP in standard form, apply the standard dual, and simplify. A free variable becomes \(x_i = x_i^+ - x_i^-\) with \(x_i^\pm \ge 0\); an equality becomes two opposite inequalities; a “≤” constraint in a minimisation is multiplied by −1; maximising \(z\) is minimising \(-z\). At the end, the pairs of non-negative variables recombine into free variables, and pairs of opposite inequalities into equalities.
Sensible–Odd–Bizarre (SOB) rules
A mnemonic that maps each non-standard element directly to its dual counterpart (table below). Quick to apply, easy to misapply, and hard to debug — not recommended as a derivation method unless you are already proficient.
Type Constraint of the min problem ↔ variable of the max problem Variable of the min problem ↔ constraint of the max problem
Sensible “≥” ↔ \(y_j \ge 0\) \(x_i \ge 0\) ↔ “≤”
Odd “=” ↔ \(y_j\) free \(x_i\) free ↔ “=”
Bizarre “≤” ↔ \(y_j \le 0\) \(x_i \le 0\) ↔ “≥”

Sensible maps to sensible, odd to odd, bizarre to bizarre, in either direction. In a minimisation the sensible constraint is “≥”; in a maximisation it is “≤”. Non-negative variables are sensible in both.

Worked example

$$\begin{aligned} \min_{x_1, x_2}\ & 5x_1 - 3x_2 \\ \text{s.t. }\ & 2x_1 + 5x_2 \ge -120 \\ & 7x_1 + 3x_2 \le 100 \\ & x_1 \ge 0, \quad x_2 \text{ free} \end{aligned}$$
Show the derivation

Write all constraints as “≤ 0”, with one multiplier each:

$$-120 - 2x_1 - 5x_2 \le 0\ \ (:\mu_1 \ge 0), \qquad 7x_1 + 3x_2 - 100 \le 0\ \ (:\mu_2 \ge 0), \qquad -x_1 \le 0\ \ (:s \ge 0)$$

Regroup the Lagrangian by variable:

$$\mathcal{L} = -120\mu_1 - 100\mu_2 + \big(5 - 2\mu_1 + 7\mu_2 - s\big)\, x_1 + \big(-3 - 5\mu_1 + 3\mu_2\big)\, x_2$$

Both coefficients must vanish for \(q\) to be finite. Eliminating \(s \ge 0\) from the first gives the dual:

$$\max_{\mu_1, \mu_2 \ge 0}\ {-120\mu_1} - 100\mu_2 \quad \text{s.t.} \quad 2\mu_1 - 7\mu_2 \le 5, \qquad 5\mu_1 - 3\mu_2 = -3$$

The dual becomes \(\max\ ({-120 y_1} + 100 y_2)\) s.t. \(2y_1 + 7y_2 \le 5\) (sensible \(x_1 \ge 0\)) and \(5y_1 + 3y_2 = -3\) (odd, \(x_2\) free) — the dual you would write directly from \(b^\top y\) and \(A^\top y\).

Strong duality check. The primal optimum is \(x^* = (0, 100/3)\) with \(p^* = -100\); the dual optimum is \(\mu^* = (0, 1)\) with \(d^* = -100\).

LP duality properties

Weak duality
If \(\hat x\) is feasible for the primal (minimisation) and \(\hat y\) for its dual, then \(c^\top \hat x \ge b^\top \hat y\): dual solutions bound the primal optimum from below. Valid for any optimization problem.
Strong duality
If the primal LP has an optimal solution \(x^*\), the dual has an optimal solution \(y^*\), and \(c^\top x^* = b^\top y^*\). All constraints are linear, so Slater's weak condition reduces to feasibility. Beyond LPs, it holds only for a certain class of problems — convex ones that satisfy Slater's condition.

The remaining cases follow from weak duality: if the primal is unbounded, the dual is infeasible; if the primal is infeasible, the dual is either infeasible or unbounded.

6Q2 — KKT conditions: characterising the optimum

Q2 — can we explicitly characterise the optimal solutions? The global bound certifies a candidate, but it does not produce one or describe it. Under strong duality, the dual problem — convex and unconstrained in \(x\) — leads to conditions that any optimum must satisfy, and that can be solved or analysed directly.

What the global bound does — and does not — do

It does It does not
Certify a candidate. A solution whose cost equals the bound is optimal. Propose a candidate. A bound rules things out; it never hands you an \(x\) to check in the first place.
Quantify how far off you are. If they do not match, the gap is an upper limit on what you leave on the table. Characterise the optimum explicitly. It is one number; it says nothing about where \(x^*\) sits or what it looks like.
Work without convexity — weakly. The bound is always valid; only its tightness depends on the problem class.
Reminder

Under strong duality the primal and dual Lagrange problems have the same optimal value. Deriving optimality conditions for the dual problem, where the inner problem in \(x\) is convex and unconstrained, is much easier than for the constrained primal — so we derive necessary and sufficient global optimality conditions there instead.

First-order condition: why the gradients line up

For fixed multipliers, the inner problem \(\min_x \mathcal{L}(x,\mu)\) is unconstrained, so its first-order condition applies. At an optimum:

First-order optimality condition (necessary) $$x^* \text{ optimal} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \nabla_x \mathcal{L}(x^*, \mu^*) = \nabla_x f(x^*) + \mu^{*\top} \nabla_x g(x^*) = 0$$

Geometrically, take the smallest case where you can see it — two variables and one constraint:

  1. \(\nabla f(x)\) is perpendicular to the contour of \(f\) through \(x\) and points where \(f\) grows fastest; the gradient of the constraint is perpendicular to its boundary.
  2. If the contour of \(f\) crosses the constraint at \(x\), then \(\nabla f\) has a component along it: you can slide along the constraint, stay feasible, and improve.
  3. At the optimum that component must be zero: the contour is tangent to the constraint, and the two gradients are parallel.

Working example, \(\min x_1 + x_2\) over the unit disc. Grey dashed lines are contours of \(f\); \(f\) decreases towards the bottom-left. At the green point the contour crosses the boundary, and the green arrow is an improving feasible step. At \(x^*\) the red contour is tangent to the boundary and the gradients are parallel.

This local condition characterises the optimum with respect to the constraints that shape it. Parallel means equal up to a scalar — and that scalar is the multiplier, one per constraint. For an equality constraint, with \(\mathcal{L} = f + \lambda h\): \(\nabla f(x^*) = -\lambda^* \nabla h(x^*)\).

Notation — sign of the equality multiplier

The slides draw the tangency condition as \(\nabla f = \lambda \nabla h\). For an equality, the sign of \(\lambda\) is a convention; these notes keep the sign that follows from the course Lagrangian \(\mathcal{L} = f + \lambda^\top h + \mu^\top g\), i.e. \(\nabla f = -\lambda \nabla h\), so that the sensitivities of section 7 come out with the stated signs. For inequalities the sign is not a convention — see below.

Is the first-order condition sufficient? No.

\(\nabla_x \mathcal{L} = 0\) alone does not guarantee optimality: it ignores whether \(x^*\) is feasible, whether the multipliers are admissible, and whether each constraint is actually active. An inequality \(g(x) \le 0\) can be in one of two states at the optimum:

Non-binding constraint

\(g(x^*) < 0\)

\(x^*\) lies strictly inside this constraint, which therefore plays no local role. The first-order condition must hold without it, so \(\mu^* = 0\): the optimal value is not sensitive to it.
Binding constraint

\(g(x^*) = 0\)

\(\nabla f(x^*) = -\mu^* \nabla g(x^*)\) with \(\mu^* \ge 0\). Since \(\nabla g\) points out of the feasible set, \(\nabla f\) must point into it — otherwise moving inside would lower \(f\). With a single binding constraint, \(\mu^* = \|\nabla f(x^*)\| / \|\nabla g(x^*)\|\).

Both cases are captured by complementary slackness, written compactly as an orthogonality condition:

$$\mu^* \perp g(x^*) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \mu^{*\top} g(x^*) = \sum_{j=1}^{J} \mu_j^*\, g_j(x^*) = 0$$
From one equation to one per constraint

Orthogonality only says that the sum is zero. But every term has the same sign: \(\mu_j^* \ge 0\) (dual feasibility) and \(g_j(x^*) \le 0\) (primal feasibility), so \(\mu_j^* g_j(x^*) \le 0\) for every \(j\). A sum of non-positive terms is zero only if each term is zero:

$$\mu_j^*\, g_j(x^*) = 0, \qquad \forall j = 1,\dots,J.$$

For each constraint, at least one factor is zero: a constraint with slack has a zero multiplier, and a positive multiplier requires a binding constraint. Both can be zero at once — a constraint can bind with a zero multiplier.

The Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) conditions

Collecting the four ingredients for the general problem, with equalities \(h_i(x) = 0\) \((: \lambda_i)\) and inequalities \(g_j(x) \le 0\) \((: \mu_j)\):

1st-order conditions \(\displaystyle \nabla_x \mathcal{L}(x^*,\lambda^*,\mu^*) = \nabla f(x^*) + \sum_{i=1}^{I} \lambda_i^* \nabla h_i(x^*) + \sum_{j=1}^{J} \mu_j^* \nabla g_j(x^*) = 0\)
Complementarity slackness conditions \(\displaystyle \mu_j^*\, g_j(x^*) = 0, \quad \forall j\)
Primal feasibility conditions \(\displaystyle h_i(x^*) = 0, \ \ \forall i, \qquad g_j(x^*) \le 0, \ \ \forall j\)
Dual feasibility conditions \(\displaystyle \mu_j^* \ge 0, \ \ \forall j, \qquad \lambda_i^* \text{ free}, \ \ \forall i\)

The first-order conditions are one equation per primal variable \(x_k\), \(k = 1,\dots,N\):

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}(x^*,\lambda^*,\mu^*)}{\partial x_k} = \frac{\partial f(x^*)}{\partial x_k} + \sum_{i} \lambda_i^* \frac{\partial h_i(x^*)}{\partial x_k} + \sum_{j} \mu_j^* \frac{\partial g_j(x^*)}{\partial x_k} = 0$$

When are they necessary, and when sufficient?

The slides summarise it in one line: the KKT conditions are necessary and sufficient if strong duality holds — for the convex problems of this course. In more detail:

The KKT conditions are… Assumptions Consequence
necessary Strong duality holds, and \(x^*\), \((\lambda^*,\mu^*)\) are primal and dual optimal (with \(f\), \(g\), \(h\) differentiable) The pair satisfies the KKT conditions.
sufficient The problem is convex Any \(\tilde x\), \((\tilde\lambda,\tilde\mu)\) satisfying them are primal and dual optimal, with zero duality gap.
necessary and sufficient The problem is convex and satisfies Slater's condition \(x^*\), \((\lambda^*,\mu^*)\) are optimal if and only if they satisfy the KKT conditions.

The ED, ED with ATC and DC-OPF are in the last row whenever they are feasible. For non-convex problems such as the AC-OPF, KKT points are only candidates — which is all a non-linear solver can promise.

Go deeper: why strong duality makes the KKT conditions necessary, and convexity sufficient

Let \(x^*\) and \((\lambda^*,\mu^*)\) be optimal with \(p^* = d^*\). Then

$$f(x^*) = q(\lambda^*,\mu^*) = \min_x \mathcal{L}(x,\lambda^*,\mu^*) \le \mathcal{L}(x^*,\lambda^*,\mu^*) = f(x^*) + \sum_i \lambda_i^* \underbrace{h_i(x^*)}_{=\,0} + \sum_j \underbrace{\mu_j^* g_j(x^*)}_{\le\, 0} \le f(x^*)$$

so every inequality is an equality. The last one forces \(\sum_j \mu_j^* g_j(x^*) = 0\), hence complementary slackness term by term, as above. The first one says that \(x^*\) minimises \(\mathcal{L}(\cdot,\lambda^*,\mu^*)\) over \(\mathbb{R}^N\), so the gradient vanishes there — the first-order conditions. Primal and dual feasibility hold by assumption.

Conversely, if the problem is convex and \(\tilde\mu \ge 0\), then \(\mathcal{L}(\cdot,\tilde\lambda,\tilde\mu)\) is convex in \(x\) (the \(h_i\) are affine, the \(g_j\) convex), so the first-order condition makes \(\tilde x\) its global minimiser. Complementary slackness then gives \(q(\tilde\lambda,\tilde\mu) = \mathcal{L}(\tilde x,\tilde\lambda,\tilde\mu) = f(\tilde x)\): the optimality certificate of section 4.

Recap: how to derive the KKT conditions

$$\begin{aligned} p^* = \min_{x \in \mathbb{R}^N}\ & f(x) \\ \text{s.t. }\ & h_i(x) = 0, \quad \forall i = 1,\dots,I && : \lambda_i \\ & g_j(x) \le 0, \quad \forall j = 1,\dots,J && : \mu_j \end{aligned}$$

  1. Associate one dual variable (Lagrange multiplier) per constraint

    \(\mu_j \ge 0\) for each inequality written as \(g_j(x) \le 0\); \(\lambda_i\) free for each equality written as \(h_i(x) = 0\).

  2. Define the Lagrangian

    \(\mathcal{L}(x,\lambda,\mu) = f(x)\) \(+ \sum_i \lambda_i h_i(x)\) \(+ \sum_j \mu_j g_j(x)\) — a relaxation of the primal problem in which the constraints are removed and penalised in the objective.

  3. Formulate the KKT conditions

    First-order conditions (one per primal variable), complementarity slackness conditions (one per inequality), primal feasibility conditions (the original constraints) and dual feasibility conditions (the signs of the multipliers).

  4. Check what they are worth — then solve or analyse them

    Is the problem convex, and does it satisfy Slater's condition? Then any solution of the KKT conditions is optimal, and every optimum satisfies them. Solve them directly, or reason case by case on which constraints bind.

Working example: solving the KKT conditions

$$1 + 2\mu x_1 = 0, \quad 1 + 2\mu x_2 = 0, \quad \mu\,(x_1^2 + x_2^2 - 1) = 0, \quad x_1^2 + x_2^2 \le 1, \quad \mu \ge 0$$

If \(\mu = 0\), the first condition reads \(1 = 0\), which is impossible — so the constraint must bind. Then \(x_1 = x_2 = -1/(2\mu)\) and \(x_1^2 + x_2^2 = 1\) give \(\mu^2 = 1/2\); dual feasibility selects \(\mu^* = 1/\sqrt{2}\), hence \(x^* = (-1/\sqrt{2}, -1/\sqrt{2})\). The problem is convex and satisfies Slater's condition, so this is the optimum — the same pair found through the dual in section 4. Geometrically, \(\nabla f(x^*) = (1,1)\) and \(\nabla g(x^*) = (-\sqrt{2}, -\sqrt{2})\), so \(\nabla f(x^*) = -\mu^* \nabla g(x^*)\) with \(\mu^* = \|\nabla f\| / \|\nabla g\| = \sqrt{2}/2\).

KKT conditions of the economic dispatch

Apply the recipe to the ED of section 5, with the same multipliers: \(\lambda\) on the balance, \(\underline{\mu}_g\) and \(\overline{\mu}_g\) on the bounds of generator \(g\).

Primal: economic dispatch $$\begin{aligned} \min_{p}\ & \sum_{g=1}^{G} c_g\, p_g \\ \text{s.t. }\ & L - \sum_{g=1}^{G} p_g = 0 && : \lambda \\ & {-p_g} \le 0, \quad \forall g && : \underline{\mu}_g \\ & p_g - \overline{P}_g \le 0, \quad \forall g && : \overline{\mu}_g \end{aligned}$$

The Lagrangian is the one of step 2 in section 5. Differentiating it with respect to each \(p_g\) gives the first-order conditions — exactly the coefficient condition that made the dual function finite:

1st-order conditions \(\displaystyle \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial p_g} = c_g - \lambda - \underline{\mu}_g + \overline{\mu}_g = 0, \quad \forall g\)
Complementarity slackness conditions \(\displaystyle \underline{\mu}_g\, p_g = 0, \qquad \overline{\mu}_g\, \big(p_g - \overline{P}_g\big) = 0, \quad \forall g\)
Primal feasibility conditions \(\displaystyle L - \sum_{g} p_g = 0, \qquad 0 \le p_g \le \overline{P}_g, \quad \forall g\)
Dual feasibility conditions \(\displaystyle \underline{\mu}_g \ge 0, \quad \overline{\mu}_g \ge 0, \quad \forall g, \qquad \lambda \text{ free}\)

Complementary slackness for the lower bound reads \(\underline{\mu}_g \cdot (-p_g) = 0\), i.e. \(\underline{\mu}_g\, p_g = 0\).

Necessary and sufficient. The ED is an LP: convex, with affine constraints, so Slater's condition reduces to feasibility, \(0 \le L \le \sum_g \overline{P}_g\). Any dispatch and multipliers that satisfy these conditions are optimal, and every optimum satisfies them.

Analysing them: the merit order

Complementary slackness sorts the generators by the bound they sit at. Combined with the first-order condition \(\overline{\mu}_g - \underline{\mu}_g = \lambda - c_g\):

Generator status Complementary slackness First-order condition gives So…
between its bounds, \(0 < p_g < \overline{P}_g\) \(\underline{\mu}_g = \overline{\mu}_g = 0\) \(c_g = \lambda\) the marginal unit sets λ
at capacity, \(p_g = \overline{P}_g > 0\) \(\underline{\mu}_g = 0\) \(\overline{\mu}_g = \lambda - c_g \ge 0\) \(c_g \le \lambda\)
off, \(p_g = 0 < \overline{P}_g\) \(\overline{\mu}_g = 0\) \(\underline{\mu}_g = c_g - \lambda \ge 0\) \(c_g \ge \lambda\)

Read in reverse: a unit cheaper than \(\lambda\) must have \(\overline{\mu}_g > 0\), hence run at capacity; a unit more expensive than \(\lambda\) must have \(\underline{\mu}_g > 0\), hence be off; only units with \(c_g = \lambda\) can be in between. Units are therefore loaded in increasing order of cost until the load is met — the merit order — and \(\lambda\) equals the cost of the unit that is partially loaded.

Solve or analyse
For small problems, the KKT conditions can be solved directly (as in the working example). For structured problems like the ED, analysing them case by case is often more useful: it explains why the solution looks the way it does.
Going further: the ED with quadratic costs

With quadratic production costs \(C_g(p_g) = \gamma_g + \beta_g p_g + \alpha_g p_g^2\), the only change is in the first-order conditions:

$$\beta_g + 2\alpha_g\, p_g - \lambda - \underline{\mu}_g + \overline{\mu}_g = 0, \qquad \forall g$$

If every \(\alpha_g \ge 0\), the problem is convex with affine constraints, so the KKT conditions remain necessary and sufficient. Every unit strictly between its bounds now runs where its marginal cost equals the same value: \(\beta_g + 2\alpha_g p_g = \lambda\). Units at capacity have \(\beta_g + 2\alpha_g \overline{P}_g \le \lambda\); units at zero have \(\beta_g \ge \lambda\).

What the KKT conditions do — and do not — do

They do They do not, on their own
Propose a candidate. Solving them produces \(x^*\) together with its multipliers. Say what the multipliers are worth. They return numbers \(\lambda^*, \mu^*\) without telling you what those numbers measure.
Characterise the optimum explicitly. They say which constraints bind and how the optimum depends on them — the merit order is an example. Quantify the impact of a change. How much would the optimal cost move if a limit or the load changed?

Answering the right-hand column is Q3.

7Q3 — Multipliers as sensitivities

Q3 — can we measure how much each constraint shapes the solutions? The multipliers say what one unit of a constraint is worth to the objective. This is what Gurobi's Pi reported in the homework — and the reading comes with conditions.

Perturb the right-hand side of each constraint

Perturbed problem and its optimal value $$p^*(u, v) = \min_{x}\ f(x) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad g_j(x) \le u_j, \ \ \forall j, \qquad h_i(x) = v_i, \ \ \forall i$$

\(u_j > 0\) relaxes inequality \(j\) and \(u_j < 0\) tightens it; \(p^*(0,0) = p^*\) is the original problem.

A global inequality

$$p^*(u, v) \ \ge\ p^*(0, 0) - \mu^{*\top} u - \lambda^{*\top} v$$

Why. For any \(x\) feasible for the perturbed problem, weak duality-style reasoning gives \(q(\lambda^*,\mu^*)\) \(\le f(x) + \lambda^{*\top} h(x) + \mu^{*\top} g(x)\) \(\le f(x) + \lambda^{*\top} v + \mu^{*\top} u\), using \(h(x) = v\), \(g(x) \le u\) and \(\mu^* \ge 0\). This inequality always holds; replacing \(q(\lambda^*,\mu^*)\) by \(p^*(0,0)\) uses strong duality. Minimising over such \(x\) gives the result.

  • Tightening a constraint (\(u_j < 0\)) raises the optimal cost by at least \(\mu_j^* |u_j|\) — a lot, if \(\mu_j^*\) is large.
  • Relaxing it (\(u_j > 0\)) lowers the cost by at most \(\mu_j^* u_j\).
  • A constraint with \(\mu_j^* = 0\) cannot lower the cost when relaxed; if it is non-binding, tightening it slightly changes nothing either.

Local sensitivity

If, in addition, \(p^*(u,v)\) is differentiable at \((0,0)\):

$$\frac{\partial p^*}{\partial u_j}(0,0) = -\mu_j^*, \qquad \frac{\partial p^*}{\partial v_i}(0,0) = -\lambda_i^*$$

Each multiplier is, up to its sign, the rate at which the optimal objective changes when the right-hand side of its constraint changes — the marginal value of the constraint. In the working example, relaxing the disc to \(x_1^2 + x_2^2 \le 1 + u\) gives \(p^*(u) = -\sqrt{2(1+u)}\), whose derivative at \(u = 0\) is \(-1/\sqrt{2} = -\mu^*\).

Reading the size of a multiplier

Zero multiplier
\(\nabla f\) is already zero along the constraint: the constraint is not shaping (limiting) \(x^*\). Removing it, or moving it slightly, does not change the solution.
Larger multiplier
\(\nabla f\) is long compared with the constraint's gradient: the objective is pushing hard against this constraint, and it is the one shaping \(x^*\). Moving it changes the optimal cost at a rate equal to the multiplier.

When the marginal reading is valid

1 · Strong duality
The problem is convex and satisfies Slater's condition, so the multipliers are those of the dual.
2 · Differentiability
At a breakpoint of \(p^*\), only one-sided slopes exist, and the multiplier is not unique: any value between the two slopes solves the dual.
3 · Marginal changes
The reading holds only until the set of binding constraints changes. Beyond that, re-solve.

Signs follow the way the constraint is written

Read the sign off the Lagrangian. In the ED, the balance is written \(h = L - \sum_g p_g = 0\), so the perturbed balance is \(L - \sum_g p_g = v\). Serving one more kWh of load (\(\sum_g p_g = L + 1\)) means \(v = -1\), hence \(\Delta z^* = -\lambda^* \cdot (-1) = +\lambda^*\): one more kWh costs \(\lambda^* = 70\) DKK at \(L = 200\) — the marginal production cost of G1.

Similarly, raising G2's capacity by 1 kW relaxes \(p_2 - \overline{P}_2 \le 0\) by \(u = +1\), so \(\Delta z^* = -\overline{\mu}_2^* = -55\) DKK: G2 produces one more kWh and G1 one less, saving \(70 - 15 = 55\) DKK.

Reading Gurobi's Pi

Pi is the rate of change of the optimal objective with respect to the right-hand side of the constraint as written in your code. For a “≤” constraint in a minimisation, that is \(-\mu^* \le 0\); on the ED balance equation written as above, it is \(\lambda^*\). For an equality, the sign depends on which side of the equation you put the constants.

The same three objects — the Lagrangian, the dual problem and the KKT conditions — answer all three questions. Which route you take depends on the question; whether the answer can be trusted depends on the problem.

Takeaways

  1. Duality answers three questions a primal solution cannot: can I trust it (a global lower bound), what characterises it (the KKT conditions), and what each constraint is worth (the multipliers).
  2. Necessary conditions screen candidates; sufficient conditions certify them. For unconstrained problems, \(\nabla f = 0\) with \(\nabla^2 f \succeq 0\) is necessary, and \(\nabla f = 0\) with \(\nabla^2 f \succ 0\) is sufficient — but only locally, unless the problem is convex.
  3. A problem is convex when its objective is convex, its inequality functions convex, and its equality functions affine. Then every local optimum is global. The ED, ED with ATC and DC-OPF are LPs, hence convex; the AC-OPF and unit commitment are not.
  4. The Lagrangian removes the constraints and charges for them, with one multiplier per constraint: free for equalities written as “= 0”, non-negative for inequalities written as “≤ 0”.
  5. Swapping min and max gives the dual problem. Its value is always a lower bound (weak duality); under convexity and Slater's condition it equals the primal optimum (strong duality), and a primal–dual pair with equal values certifies optimality.
  6. LP duality is Lagrange duality with the inner minimisation solved by hand. Both routes — and the direct construction from combinations of constraints — give the same dual.
  7. The KKT conditions — first-order, complementarity slackness, primal feasibility, dual feasibility — are necessary under strong duality and sufficient for convex problems. Complementary slackness holds term by term, because every term has the same sign. For the ED they are the merit order.
  8. Multipliers are sensitivities: \(\partial p^*/\partial u_j = -\mu_j^*\) and \(\partial p^*/\partial v_i = -\lambda_i^*\) — valid under strong duality, where \(p^*\) is differentiable, and for changes that keep the binding set. Signs follow the way each constraint is written.
