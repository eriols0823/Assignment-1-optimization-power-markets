Up to 10 kWh every extra kWh is bought in hour 1 at 20 (the cheapest hour has 10 kW of room) and sold in hour 4 at 80: slope 60, and δ̄ = 60 sits in hour 1, where the battery is full. From 10 to 20 kWh hour 1 and hour 4 are at the power limit, so the extra kWh is bought in hour 2 at 30 and sold in hour 3 at 60: slope 30, δ̄2 = 30. Above 20 kWh the two cheap hours cannot charge more than 2 × 10 = 20 kWh: the power limit takes over, the battery is never full, Σ δ̄ = 0 and the profit is flat at 900.

Up to 7.5 kW the battery is never full (2 P̄ ≤ 15): each extra kW buys one kWh more in hours 1 and 2 (20 + 30) and sells one more in hours 3 and 4 (60 + 80), slope 140 − 50 = 90 = Σ μ̄ (all four hours at P̄). From 7.5 to 15 kW the capacity binds (15 kWh): an extra kW moves one kWh from the 30-hour to the 20-hour and one from the 60-hour to the 80-hour, slope 10 + 20 = 30 = μ̄ch1 + μ̄dis4. Above 15 kW the whole 15 kWh is bought at 20 and sold at 80 in one hour each: nothing is at the power limit, Σ μ̄ = 0, profit 900.

c. Σ δ̄ = 30 > 20 DKK per kWh per day, so a kWh pays; Σ μ̄ = 30 < 40 per kW, so a kW does not: add capacity first. It holds until the next kink at Ē = 20 kWh, where Σ δ̄ drops to 0 and no further kWh pays (at P̄ = 10 kW). It stops holding because the multipliers are slopes of a piecewise-linear profit: at a kink another constraint — here the power limit — becomes binding and the sensitivity changes. (And after adding kWh the value of a kW changes too: at 20 kWh Σ μ̄ has risen to 90, so kW now pay — the two decisions interact, see Q4.)

The profit per kWh is flat at 121 up to 10 kWh and falls from there, while the profit keeps rising to 60 kWh: the average falls as soon as the marginal value (Σ δ̄, the slope) drops below the average — each kink lowers the slope, so every kWh after the first ten earns less than the ones before it. Sizing on "profit per kWh" and sizing on "one more kWh pays" are different questions; the multiplier answers the second.

b. The class day has a block length H = 6 h (median run of above-/below-mean hours), so H · P̄ = 60 kWh is the most the battery can move in one block at 10 kW — that is the last kink, above which the profit is flat. The kinks at 10, 20, 30, 40, 50 kWh are multiples of P̄ × 1 h: each extra 10 kWh lets the battery use one more hour of the cheap block and one more hour of the expensive block at full power, and each pair of hours is less favourable than the previous one (the prices within a block are not flat), which is why the slope falls in steps of 10 kWh rather than staying constant until H · P̄.

c. At Ē = 25 kWh, Σ μ̄ falls in steps (417 up to 4.8 kW, 242, 138, 63 up to 12.5 kW, 15 up to 25 kW) and reaches zero at P̄ = 25 kW, where D = Ē/P̄ = 1 h: the battery can fill or empty completely in a single hour, so no power bound is ever tight — it buys its 25 kWh in the cheapest hour and sells them in the dearest, and the profit is flat at 3 025 above that.

One evening peak, sweep Ē at 10 kW: the profit rises until Ē = 80 kWh (3 600 DKK) and is flat above; the segments have slopes 70 (0–20 kWh: bought at 20, sold at 90 in hours 20–21), 60 (sold at 80), 55 (75), 40 (60), 35 (55), and 15 on 60–80 kWh (the peak is used up at 6 × 10 kW = 60 kWh; the extra kWh is sold in the day hours at 35), which is Σ δ̄ there; above 80 kWh the eight night hours at 10 kW cannot charge more. Square wave, sweep P̄ at 25 kWh: the profit rises at 720 DKK per kW until P̄ = 12.5 kW (9 000 DKK) and is flat above; the whole rising part is one segment of slope 720.

c. The evening peak lasts six hours (60 to 90 DKK/kWh) after eight cheap night hours at 20: at 10 kW the battery can sell 60 kWh in the peak, and even the day hours at 35 still pay 15 over the night price, so the profit keeps rising until the eight night hours are used to the full — 80 kWh. A long price event rewards energy: the maximum state of charge matters most when the dear (or cheap) hours are many. The square wave alternates every two hours: only 20 kWh can be sold before the price drops again, so 25 kWh is already more than enough and every extra kW sells more in each two-hour window — a short, frequent price event rewards power, and the maximum power matters most when the price changes often.

d. With H = 8 h (25 kWh / 10 kW): profit 1 500, Σ δ̄ = 60, Σ μ̄ = 0 — energy matters now. The blocks are four times longer than the duration D = 2.5 h, so the battery empties long before the dear block ends; the power limit is never the constraint that stops a sale, the capacity is. The same battery is power-limited on a 2-hour wave and energy-limited on an 8-hour wave: the sizing signal depends on the price profile, not on the battery alone.

4. Q4 — Answer

a. One more kWh pays while Σ δ̄ > 25: 31 on 40–50 kWh, 20 on 50–60 kWh, so up to Ē = 50 kWh (D = 5 h at 10 kW), where the profit is 3 970 DKK against a daily capacity cost of 1 250.

b. At 50 kWh / 10 kW, Σ μ̄ = 297 DKK per kW — far above 50: a kW pays handsomely. At the preset (25 kWh) it was 63. Adding energy raised the value of power: with 50 kWh to move, the battery is at its power limit in many more hours, so each kW of headroom is used more often. The two capacities are complements; the multiplier of one depends on the other.

c. 25 × 82 + 10 × 63 = 2 050 + 630 = 2 680 = the profit (strong duality: with E0 = 0 and E̲end = 0 the dual objective is Ē Σ δ̄ + P̄ Σ μ̄). The identity says the profit is exactly the rents of the two capacities and, since the LP is linear in (Ē, P̄) at a fixed duration, doubling both doubles the profit. So the multipliers decide the shape of the battery — the duration D, i.e. the ratio of kWh to kW at which one more of each just pays its cost — but not its size: for a price-taker with constant returns to scale, the profitable size is either zero or as large as a budget, a site or the price impact of the battery allows (which is where the price-taker assumption of section 3 of the notes stops being true). Deciding Ē and P̄ one at a time therefore never ends: each addition raises the other's multiplier.
