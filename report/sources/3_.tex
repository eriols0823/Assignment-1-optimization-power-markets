\subsection{(a) Formulation with a minimum daily energy}

\subsubsection{New input data and constraint}
Starting from the model of Question~2.(c), the consumer must now consume at least a minimum
total energy $\Emin$ (kWh) over the whole day. This is the only new input datum, and it adds a
single new constraint:
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{t \in \T} \load \geq \Emin.
    \label{eq:c_min_energy}
\end{equation}
No new decision variables are needed.
\subsubsection{Daily problem}
The daily problem keeps the objective of Question~2 and all the constraints of Question~1, and
adds \eqref{eq:c_min_energy}. With $D_t$ the disutility of the deviation of the load from the
reference $\lref$ (in Question~3, the quadratic disutility of 2.(c)):
\begin{align}
    \max_{\{\load, \qimp, \qexp, \pvprod\}_{t \in \T}} \quad
    & \sum_{t \in \T} \Big( - D_t(\load) - \pimp \, \qimp + \pexp \, \qexp - \cpv \, \pvprod \Big)
    \label{eq:q3_objective} \\
    \text{s.t.} \quad
    & \load = \pvprod + \qimp - \qexp, & \forall t \in \T, \nonumber \\
    & \Lmin \leq \load \leq \Lmax, & \forall t \in \T, \nonumber \\
    & 0 \leq \pvprod \leq \PVmax, & \forall t \in \T, \nonumber \\
    & \qimp \geq 0, \quad \qexp \geq 0, & \forall t \in \T, \nonumber \\
    & \sum_{t \in \T} \load \geq \Emin. & \nonumber
\end{align}

\subsubsection{Change in the properties}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Decomposability:} constraint \eqref{eq:c_min_energy} is the first constraint
    of the assignment that links different hours, since it involves the loads of all 24 hours. The daily problem can therefore no longer be split into 24 independent hourly
    problems: it has to be solved as a single 24-hour problem.
    \item \textbf{Problem class and convexity:}
    The objective is the same as in Question~2.(c), where the quadratic disutility gives a concave quadratic objective. The hourly constraints are affine, including  the new constraint \eqref{eq:c_min_energy}. The problem therefore remains a convex Quadratic Program (QP). So the new constraint impacts decomposability but not the convexity. 

    \item \textbf{Feasibility:}
The largest total energy the consumer can consume during the day is $24\Lmax$. The problem is therefore feasible as long as $\Emin \leq 24\Lmax$, assuming $\Lmin \leq \Lmax$ and that the original hourly constraints are feasible. The reference profile $\lref$ does not restrict feasibility, since it only appears in the objective through the disutility. The load may therefore deviate from $\lref$ if this is needed to satisfy \eqref{eq:c_min_energy}.

\end{itemize}

\subsection{(b) Lagrangian, dual problem and KKT conditions of the daily problem}

% - Strong duality and necessity/sufficiency of KKT: same route as in 1.(b) (problem class from

Following the brief, the multiplier of the minimum-energy constraint \eqref{eq:c_min_energy}
is denoted by $\muE \geq 0$. It is a multiplier of the same kind as those of Question~1.(b):
it belongs to an inequality, which in the form $g(x) \leq 0$ reads
$\Emin - \sum_{t \in \T} \load \leq 0$, and it is in DKK/kWh. Unlike the hourly multipliers, it
has no hour index, since the constraint applies to the whole day.

\subsubsection{Lagrangian}

As in Question~1.(b), we write the problem in minimization form. The objective
then becomes the negative of \eqref{eq:q3_objective}. The Lagrangian is

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}
= \sum_{t\in\T} \Big[
& D_t(\load)
+ \pimp\,\qimp
- \pexp\,\qexp
+ \cpv\,\pvprod \\
&+ \lbal(\load-\pvprod-\qimp+\qexp) \\
&+ \muLlo(\Lmin-\load)
+ \muLup(\load-\Lmax) \\
&- \muPVlo\pvprod
+ \muPVup(\pvprod-\PVmax) \\
&- \muimp\qimp
- \muexp\qexp
\Big] \\
&+ \muE
\left(
\Emin-\sum_{t\in\T}\load
\right).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:q3_lagrangian}
\end{equation}
\subsubsection{Dual problem}
The dual function is given when the dual variables are fixed and the Lagrangian is minimized. For the load, the quadratic disutility from 2 c) is $D_t(\load)=c^Q(\load-\lref)^2$. Study the Lagrangian at \eqref{eq:q3_lagrangian} and it can be seen that the terms that depend on the $\load$ are
$D_t(\load)+\lbal\load-\muLlo\load+\muLup\load-\muE\load$. To keep this notation shorter: $\beta_t=\lbal-\muLlo+\muLup-\muE$. Add both of this together and it can be seen that the load dependent part of the Lagrangian becomes $c^Q(\load-\lref)^2+\beta_t\load$.

If we minimize this with respect to $\load$ gives $2c^Q(\load-\lref)+\beta_t=0$ which imples: $\load=\lref-\beta_t/(2c^Q)$. If this value is then substituted back into the Lagrangian the load contribution becomes $\beta_t\lref-\beta_t^2/(4c^Q)$.

The remaining primal variables are linear in the Lagrangian. Their bounds have
already been included through their multipliers, so they are treated as free when forming the dual function. If one of their coefficients was non-zero, the Lagrangian could be made infinitely small. The dual function would then be $-\infty$.The required conditions are therefore
$\cpv-\lbal-\muPVlo+\muPVup=0$ for PV,
$\pimp-\lbal-\muimp=0$ for imports, and
$-\pexp+\lbal-\muexp=0$ for exports, for every $t\in\T$.

After minimizing over all primal variables, the dual function is

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
q(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\mu},\muE)
=
\muE\Emin
+
\sum_{t\in\T}
\left[
    \muLlo\Lmin
    -\muLup\Lmax
    -\muPVup\PVmax
    +\beta_t\lref
    -\frac{\beta_t^2}{4c^Q}
\right].
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:q3_dual_function}
\end{equation}

This expression is finite only when the three coefficient conditions above hold. Otherwise
$q(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\mu},\muE)=-\infty$.

The dual problem is therefore

\begin{align}
\max_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\mu},\muE}
\quad
& q(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\mu},\muE)
\label{eq:q3_dual_problem} \\
\text{s.t.}\quad
& \cpv-\lbal-\muPVlo+\muPVup=0,
&& \forall t\in\T, \nonumber\\
& \pimp-\lbal-\muimp=0,
&& \forall t\in\T, \nonumber\\
& -\pexp+\lbal-\muexp=0,
&& \forall t\in\T, \nonumber\\
& \muLlo,\muLup,\muPVlo,\muPVup,\muimp,\muexp\geq0,
&& \forall t\in\T. \nonumber
\end{align}

Also $\muE\geq0$, while $\lbal$ is free for every $t\in\T$.

\subsubsection{KKT conditions}
Primal feasibility, dual feasibility, stationarity and complementary slackness will be shown below:

\paragraph{Primal feasibility}
The primal variables must satisfy the constraints of Q3 a. So: 

$\load=\pvprod+\qimp-\qexp$, $\Lmin\leq\load\leq\Lmax$,
$0\leq\pvprod\leq\PVmax$, and $\qimp,\qexp\geq0$ for every $t\in\T$.
The daily minimum-energy requirement must also hold, so
$\sum_{t\in\T}\load\geq\Emin$.

\paragraph{Dual feasibility.}
All multipliers of inequality constraints must be non-negative. For every $t\in\T$,
the load and PV multipliers satisfy
$\muLlo,\muLup,\muPVlo,\muPVup\geq0$.
The import and export multipliers satisfy $\muimp,\muexp\geq0$, and the daily
multiplier satisfies $\muE\geq0$. The power-balance multiplier $\lbal$ is free.


\paragraph{Stationarity.}
The derivative of the Lagrangian with respect to each primal variable must be zero. This gives

\begin{align}
    \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \load}
    &= 2c^Q(\load-\lref)+\lbal-\muLlo+\muLup-\muE = 0,
    && \forall t\in\T,
    \label{eq:q3_stationarity_load}\\
    \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \pvprod}
    &= \cpv-\lbal-\muPVlo+\muPVup = 0,
    && \forall t\in\T, \nonumber\\
    \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \qimp}
    &= \pimp-\lbal-\muimp = 0,
    && \forall t\in\T, \nonumber\\
    \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \qexp}
    &= -\pexp+\lbal-\muexp = 0,
    && \forall t\in\T. \nonumber
\end{align}

\paragraph{Complementary slackness} Multiplier times constraint must be zero:
\begin{align}
    \muLlo(\Lmin-\load) &= 0,
    && \forall t\in\T, \nonumber\\
    \muLup(\load-\Lmax) &= 0,
    && \forall t\in\T, \nonumber\\
    \muPVlo\pvprod &= 0,
    && \forall t\in\T, \nonumber\\
    \muPVup(\pvprod-\PVmax) &= 0,
    && \forall t\in\T, \nonumber\\
    \muimp\qimp &= 0,
    && \forall t\in\T, \nonumber\\
    \muexp\qexp &= 0,
    && \forall t\in\T, \nonumber\\
    \muE\left(\Emin-\sum_{t\in\T}\load\right) &= 0.
    \label{eq:q3_complementary}
\end{align}

\subsubsection{Strong duality}

The formulation in 3 a of the daily problem is a convex quadratic problem and all constraints are affine. Given that all constraints are affine, the slater argument from Q1 (b) can be recycled. So for that reason strong duality holds here also. Optimal objective value is same for both primal and dual and KKT conditions are sufficient and necessary for optimality.

\subsection{(c) Expected behavior}
\paragraph{Can the consumer consume above it hourly reference?}
Yes it can. Partly because it can be forced to do so to fit its minimum daily energy demand $\Emin>\sum_{t\in\T}\lref$. This could make it such that in the cheaper hours it deviates more above the reference compared to the more expensive hours, where the reference is kept.

\paragraph{Can the consumer consume above its daily minimum requirement?}
Yes this is also possible. $\Emin$ only sets a lower bound and not an upper one. So if electricity is cheap and it increases utility more than it costs the consumer can consume above it daily requirement $\Emin$.

\subsection{(g) A small battery}

\subsubsection{(i) Formulation with a battery}
The model of (a) is kept, with the same objective and constraints, and a battery is added. Its
data are given in the repository; since the time step is one hour, a power in kW is
equivalent to an energy of kWh per hour.

\paragraph{New input data.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\Ebat$: energy capacity of the battery, 4~kWh.
    \item $\Pch$, $\Pdis$: maximum charging and discharging power, 1.5~kW each.
    \item $\etach$, $\etadis$: charging and discharging efficiencies, 0.95 each. $\etach$ is the
    fraction of the charged energy that reaches the battery, and $\etadis$ the fraction of the
    discharged energy that reaches the consumer.
    \item $\einit$: state of charge at the start of the day, 2~kWh (50\,\% of $\Ebat$).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{New decision variables.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\pch \geq 0$, $\pdis \geq 0$: energy charged into and discharged from the battery in
    hour $t$ (kWh/h), measured on the consumer's side.
    \item $\soc$: state of charge at the end of hour $t$ (kWh).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{New and modified constraints.}
The power balance of (a) now includes the battery, which acts as an extra load when charging
and as an extra source when discharging:
\begin{equation}
    \load + \pch = \pvprod + \qimp - \qexp + \pdis, \qquad \forall t \in \T.
    \label{eq:g_balance}
\end{equation}
The state of charge links each hour with the previous one, with the losses of both
conversions:
\begin{equation}
    \soc = \soc[t-1] + \etach \, \pch - \frac{\pdis}{\etadis}, \qquad \forall t \in \T,
    \qquad \text{with } \soc[-1] = \einit.
    \label{eq:g_soc}
\end{equation}
The state of charge and the power are limited by the battery data:
\begin{align}
    & 0 \leq \soc \leq \Ebat, & \forall t \in \T, \label{eq:g_cap} \\
    & 0 \leq \pch \leq \Pch, \quad 0 \leq \pdis \leq \Pdis, & \forall t \in \T. \label{eq:g_power}
\end{align}
The minimum-energy requirement \eqref{eq:c_min_energy} is unchanged: it applies to the load
$\load$, so the energy that the battery delivers to the load counts towards $\Emin$, while
the energy stored in the battery or lost in it does not.

\paragraph{State of charge at the end of the day.}
The energy left in the battery at the end of hour 23 is not lost: it can be used the following
day. We value it in the objective at the price $\piend$ it would cost to replace it the
following day, before it is needed. Let $p_{D+1,t}$ be the forecast market price of the
following day, given in the repository, and $p^{\mathrm{imp}}_{D+1,t} = p_{D+1,t} + \tauimp$
its effective import price. The stored energy is first worth using at the morning price peak
of the following day, in hour
\begin{equation}
    t^{\mathrm{peak}} = \arg\max_{t \in \{0, \dots, 11\}} p_{D+1,t},
\end{equation}
and it can be replaced in the night hours before it,
$\T^{\mathrm{night}}_{D+1} = \{0, \dots, t^{\mathrm{peak}} - 1\}$. Storing one kWh then costs
the cheapest import price of those hours, divided by $\etach$. A stored kWh is never worth
more than what it saves when it is discharged at the most expensive hour of the following day,
so
\begin{equation}
    \piend = \min \left\{
        \frac{\min_{t \in \T^{\mathrm{night}}_{D+1}} p^{\mathrm{imp}}_{D+1,t}}{\etach},
        \;\; \etadis \max_{t \in \T} p^{\mathrm{imp}}_{D+1,t}
    \right\}.
    \label{eq:g_piend}
\end{equation}
This assumes that the night hours are long enough to refill the battery,
$|\T^{\mathrm{night}}_{D+1}| \, \etach \, \Pch \geq \Ebat$.

This single price is a simplification. It gives every kWh left in the battery the same value,
while the power limit means that refilling a full battery takes several night hours, at
increasing prices, and it ignores how the battery would be used the following day. The exact
value of the energy left at the end is a decreasing curve of $\soc[23]$, which a two-day model
captures automatically. We do not use such a model as the base case because the task is to
extend the daily problem of (a), with the end of the day represented in its constraints or its
objective. We check in (v) that this simplification does not change the decisions.

The objective of (a) becomes
\begin{equation}
    \max \quad \sum_{t \in \T} \Big( - D_t(\load) - \pimp \, \qimp + \pexp \, \qexp
    - \cpv \, \pvprod \Big) + \piend \big( \soc[23] - \einit \big).
    \label{eq:g_end}
\end{equation}
The term $-\piend \einit$ is a constant and does not change the optimal decisions, but it makes
the objective count only the net change of the stored energy: if the battery ends the day as
it started, the term is zero, and if it uses part of its initial energy, it pays for it at the
replacement price. The value of the battery in (iv) and (v) is then a fair comparison with the
case without battery. In the last hours of the day, we expect the battery to discharge when
prices are high and to recharge only in the hours where storing a kWh costs less than its value
at the end, $\pimp / \etach < \piend$. Otherwise it ends the day below $\einit$, and the energy
is replaced the following night.

\paragraph{Alternatives.}
A cyclic condition $\soc[23] \geq \einit$ is the simplest choice, but it is not neutral: it
forces the battery to refill before midnight whatever the price, even when the same energy
could be replaced more cheaply the following day, and in that case it underestimates the value
of the battery.
Leaving $\soc[23]$ free has the opposite problem: the energy left at the end has no value, so
the battery empties itself without paying for its initial energy, which overestimates its
value. The last price of the day or its trend need no forecasts, but they only look at the end
of the day, while the stored energy is used the following morning. We compare these
alternatives numerically in (v).

\paragraph{Properties.}
The objective only gains a linear term and all the new constraints are affine, so the problem
is still a convex QP. It is feasible under the same condition as in (a), since leaving the
battery idle ($\pch = \pdis = 0$, $\soc = \einit$) satisfies all the battery constraints. The
state of charge \eqref{eq:g_soc} adds a second link between consecutive hours, besides $\Emin$,
so the problem still does not decompose by hour.

\paragraph{Outside the model.}
In the model, the battery does not know where its energy comes from: the balance
\eqref{eq:g_balance} mixes PV and imports, and the same $\etach$ applies to both. The only
difference between them is their price ($\cpv$ against $\pimp$). In a real installation the
losses can differ too: when the PV and the battery share a hybrid inverter (DC coupling), PV
energy is stored without being converted to AC and back, so charging from the PV loses less
than charging from the grid. Modelling this would require separate charging variables for PV
and grid energy, each with its own efficiency.

\subsubsection{(ii) Simultaneous charging and discharging}
\paragraph{With the data of this assignment.}
Charging and discharging in the same hour is never optimal. Suppose that in some hour both
$\pch > 0$ and $\pdis > 0$, and reduce them together, $\pch$ by a small $\delta > 0$ and $\pdis$ by
$\etach \etadis \, \delta$. By \eqref{eq:g_soc} the state of charge $\soc$ does not change, so
no other hour is affected. In the balance \eqref{eq:g_balance}, however, the battery now
draws $(1 - \etach \etadis)\,\delta > 0$ less energy: this is the energy that was being lost
in the round trip. That energy can be used to import less, saving $\pimp > 0$, to export
more, earning $\pexp > 0$, or to produce less PV, saving $\cpv > 0$. Exporting more is always
possible, since exports are unbounded, so the objective strictly improves in every hour,
whatever the regime. With our data $\etach \etadis = 0.9025 < 1$, all effective prices are
positive ($\pexp \geq 0.85 - 0.40 = 0.45$~DKK/kWh) and the PV can be curtailed, so wasting
energy in the battery always costs money.

\paragraph{When it becomes a real risk.}
The argument fails when losing energy is no longer a cost: when importing is paid
($\pimp < 0$), or when exporting costs money ($\pexp < 0$) and a surplus cannot be curtailed
or used by the load. Burning energy through simultaneous charging and discharging then
becomes profitable. With a lossless battery
($\etach = \etadis = 1$) the overlap costs nothing: the optimum is not unique, and the solver
may return a solution with overlap.

\paragraph{How it could be fixed.}
The exact fix is a binary variable $z_t \in \{0, 1\}$ that allows only one direction per hour,
\begin{equation}
    \pch \leq \Pch \, z_t, \qquad \pdis \leq \Pdis \, (1 - z_t), \qquad \forall t \in \T,
\end{equation}
which turns the QP into a mixed-integer QP (MIQP), harder to solve and without duals. A
cheaper mitigation is a linear degradation cost $c^{\mathrm{deg}} (\pch + \pdis)$ in the
cost, which makes every kWh through the battery more expensive. It acts like extra losses: it
removes the overlap only as long as the gain from burning energy is smaller than the cost, so
with very negative prices the overlap can still appear.

\subsubsection{(iii) Implementation}
The battery is added to the model of (d), in \texttt{FlexibleConsumerModel.build()} in the file
\texttt{src/model.py}, and only becomes active when the input data contain a battery. The value
$\piend$ is computed from the next-day data by the function \texttt{terminal\_value()}, in the
same file, so it follows the input data when they change. The base case is solved by running
\texttt{python main.py -{}-question Q3\_battery}, and the results are saved automatically in the
folder \texttt{results/}.

Running the code gives the hour-by-hour schedule, now with the charging and discharging power
and the state of charge, the objective value, $\piend$ and the value of the stored-energy change
$\piend(\soc[23] - \einit)$, and the dual variable of every constraint, including the state of
charge, whose dual is the value of a kWh stored in each hour. As for the other constraints,
the battery limits are written as constraints in the form $g(x) \leq 0$, and the state of
charge as $h(x) = 0$.

\paragraph{No overlap.} The largest value of $\min\{\pch, \pdis\}$ over the day is
$1.5 \cdot 10^{-10}$~kWh/h, which is zero up to the solver tolerance: the battery never charges
and discharges in the same hour, as argued in (ii).

\paragraph{Schedule.} The battery makes two cycles a day. It charges in the cheapest hours of the
night (3--4) and of the middle of the day (13--15), and discharges at the morning peak (8--10) and
over the evening peak (17--22). At the evening peak it even discharges more than the load needs
and sells the surplus, which is why both imports and exports increase. The battery ends the day
empty: recharging it before midnight would cost more than refilling it the following night, at
$\piend = 1.42$~DKK/kWh, as expected in (i).

\paragraph{Deviations from the reference.} The load barely changes, and the disutility drops only
slightly. In most hours the consumer imports anyway, so the value of one more kWh is simply the
import price, with or without battery, and by the stationarity condition of 3.(b) the load has
no reason to move. Only in the evening, where the battery now supplies cheaper energy, does the
load rise a little. The battery takes over the role that the flexibility of the load had in (e):
it shifts energy in time, so the load does not have to.

\paragraph{Role of the minimum-energy requirement.} $\Emin$ is still binding, with a total load
of exactly 40~kWh, but its multiplier $\mu^E$, the cost of requiring one more kWh per day,
drops from 2.37 to 2.31~DKK/kWh: the extra energy can now be supplied in part by energy bought
or produced in cheaper hours. The energy stored or lost in the
battery does not count towards $\Emin$, so the battery cannot be used to meet it on its own.

\paragraph{Value of the battery.} The net utility is minus the procurement cost, minus the
disutility, plus the value of the change of stored energy. It is negative because the consumer
always pays more than it earns, so a value closer to zero is better: it improves from $-63.31$
to $-60.04$~DKK, so the battery is worth 3.27~DKK/day. The procurement cost drops by 5.89~DKK
and the disutility by 0.22~DKK, but part of these savings comes from the 2~kWh that the battery
had at the start and uses during the day. This energy is not free, since it has to be bought
again the following night, so it is charged at $\piend$ (2.84~DKK). Without this charge the value
of the battery would be overestimated.

\subsubsection{(v) Sensitivity analysis of the value of the battery}
The value of the battery is the difference between the objective with and without the battery,
with the same data. We vary one parameter at a time and keep the rest at the base case: three
parameters of the environment (the spread of the prices around their mean and the price level,
both on the two days so that $\piend$ follows, and the disutility coefficient $c^Q$) and three
of the battery (its capacity, with $\einit$ kept at half of it, its power and its efficiencies).
All runs come from \texttt{python main.py -{}-question Q3\_battery}, and
Figure~\ref{fig:q3g_sens} shows the value of the battery in each of them.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/Q3/q3g_sensitivity.png}
    \caption{Value of the battery when one parameter changes. The dotted line is the base case
    (3.27~DKK/day).}
    \label{fig:q3g_sens}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Price spread.} This has by far the largest effect. The battery earns money by buying
energy when it is cheap and using it when it is expensive, so the larger the gap between cheap
and expensive hours, the more each cycle earns: doubling the spread more than triples the value.
Even with flat prices the battery is still worth something, because the tariffs make buying a
kWh much more expensive than selling one: the battery stores evening PV that would otherwise be
curtailed and uses it at night instead of importing.

\paragraph{Price level.} Raising all prices by the same amount barely matters. The gaps between
the hours stay the same; only the energy lost in each cycle becomes a little more expensive, so
the value drops slightly.

\paragraph{Flexibility of the consumer.} A consumer that can easily move its own load (low
$c^Q$) already shifts its consumption to the cheap hours, so the battery has little left to do.
A rigid consumer (high $c^Q$) cannot, and the battery does the shifting for it. The value of the
battery more than doubles from the most flexible to the most rigid consumer of the sweep, and
then levels off: the battery and the flexibility of the load are substitutes, as seen in (iv).

\paragraph{Capacity and power.} Both add value, but less and less: a battery twice as large or
twice as fast as the base case is worth only about 20\,\% more. A plausible explanation is that
the prices have two peaks a day; once the battery can cover both, a larger or faster battery
only reaches hours where the price differences are small. For the largest capacities the value
is also somewhat overstated, because the battery starts with half of its capacity, which our
$\piend$ values at the cheapest night price although refilling that much energy would take more
night hours at higher prices (see the end of the day below).

\paragraph{Efficiency.} The value depends strongly on the losses. Every kWh that goes through the
battery loses part of itself, so the price gap between charging and discharging has to pay for
the losses. At 90\,\% efficiency each way the value already drops by about 40\,\%, and at
80\,\% the battery is almost never used: the only hours expensive enough are late in the
evening, when the consumer barely consumes and the energy could only be exported at a lower
price. A lossless battery charges and discharges at the same time in some hours, the non-unique
case described in (ii).

\paragraph{End of the day.} We also checked the choice of (i) against a model that sees the
following morning: day $D$ and the first eleven hours of $D+1$, which fits the size limit of our
solver licence once the state of charge is written as an expression ($\Emin$ only applies to
day $D$). It takes the same decisions as our daily model.

It also gives the exact value of each kWh left at midnight. This value decreases from 1.52 to
1.42~DKK/kWh, because refilling a whole battery takes several night hours at increasing prices.
Our $\piend$ is the lowest of these values, so it gives the right schedule but overstates the
value of the battery a little, by about 5\,\%. The decisions would only change above
1.71~DKK/kWh, when keeping energy becomes better than exporting it at the evening peak.

The alternatives of (i) behave as expected. The free end reports almost twice the value, because
the battery uses its initial energy for free, and the cyclic condition forces an expensive refill
before midnight. Without forecasts of the following day, extrapolating the trend of the last two
prices works well here, since it follows the fall of the prices towards the night, although it
only sees two hours. The last price alone lies right at the limit where the decisions change.

\paragraph{Conclusion.} A battery at the consumer level is worth the most where the prices change
a lot within the day, where buying from the grid costs much more than selling to it, and where
the consumer cannot easily shift its own load. Of its technical characteristics, the efficiency
matters most, since it decides whether the price differences pay for the losses at all; extra
capacity and power only help up to what the daily price profile can use. Whether a larger
battery pays off then depends on its cost: in this case, doubling the capacity adds only
0.67~DKK per day, which would have to be compared with the cost of the extra 4~kWh over the
lifetime of the battery. In our base case the
battery is worth 3.27~DKK/day in our model, or about 3.12 when the energy left at midnight is
valued exactly. In practice the value would be somewhat lower, since the model has no
degradation cost: every cycle wears the battery, so each one would need an even larger price
spread to pay off.
